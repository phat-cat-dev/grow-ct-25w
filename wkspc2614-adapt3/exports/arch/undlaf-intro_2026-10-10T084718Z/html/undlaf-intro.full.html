Three things shape how you get on at university: how you learn, how you are assessed, and the feedback you get along the way. Some students meet these one at a time and treat them as separate hurdles. This course proposes a different view. It shows how learning, assessment and feedback work together as one cycle, one you will meet again and again throughout your studies. Understanding how these parts of academic studies connect is worth your attention right at the start, because it lets you study with purpose from the outset rather than spend your first terms working it out the hard way.

Over five short lessons you will look at what learning at university actually asks of you, and at how university spells out what you are meant to learn. You will then look at what assessment is really for, at how to make feedback worth having, and finally at how the three connect into a single cycle you can run deliberately. None of it is abstract: it all applies to the work you are already doing, this term, in whatever you are studying. By the end you will have a clearer picture of how your own studies work, and a few practical habits for making each piece of work feed the next.

block · L01-b-15 · trackingId=9 · skeletonKey=concept-definition

(text) component · L01-c-15 · text · layout=full · classes=""

A discourse community is a group of people who share a way of making and testing knowledge: how they ask questions, what counts as good evidence, and how they reach agreement. Every subject at university is its own discourse community, with its own habits of argument.

Learning here is less about collecting facts and more about joining that community. You learn how your subject thinks, not only what it has concluded. This matters because it changes what getting better means: progress is not storing more information, but taking a fuller part in how your subject builds knowledge.

Worked example component · L01-c-30 · text · layout=full · classes="slot__L01__worked-example"

⟦ CXP slot: page=L01 slot=worked-example — subject-filled at merge ⟧

Consider two psychology students preparing for the same seminar on the same journal article.

Tom reads the article once and highlights the findings, ready to repeat them if asked. The study has been covered and the results are in his notes. This is surface learning: the information has been taken in, but it sits on its own, and he treats the paper as a set of facts to recall.

Amara reads the same article and keeps asking why. Why did the authors choose this design? How does the sample limit what the findings can claim? Where would a different theory predict something else? By the seminar, Amara can connect the study to a wider debate about method and evidence, and take a reasoned position within it. This is deep learning: the ideas are linked into understanding she can use and defend.

Both students have read the article. Only Amara has taken the step psychology's discourse community values, turning reported findings into a judgement about how good the evidence is.

Understanding learning outcomes article · L02-a-02 · classes="article--lesson-concept"

block · L02-b-15 · trackingId=20 · skeletonKey=concept-definition

(text) component · L02-c-15 · text · layout=full · classes=""

A learning outcome is a statement of what you should be able to do by the end of a module. It describes what you will be able to demonstrate, not just what will be covered in class. Every module is built around a set of these, usually printed in the module handbook or on its page in your virtual learning environment.

They matter because your assessment is, in effect, asking you to evidence and demonstrate them. The outcome tells you what you are aiming at; the assessment is where you show you have reached it. Learning to read an outcome closely is one of the most useful habits you can build early, because it tells you what success actually looks like.

Inside a learning outcome component · L02-c-25 · accordion · layout=full · classes=""

Select each part to open it. Work through all of them.

A learning outcome is short, but it has parts. Open each one to see what it is doing.

Item 1: The active verb

Every outcome turns on a verb: analyse, evaluate, describe, apply. The verb tells you the kind of thinking you must show, and it is usually the first thing to find.

Item 2: What it is about

After the verb comes the subject of the outcome: the material, skill or situation the verb acts on. This tells you where to point your effort.

Item 3: How you demonstrate it

An outcome is only met when you show it. Ask what would count as evidence: a judgement reached, a method applied, a case explained. This is what your assessment will look for.

Item 4: Why it matters

Read this way, an outcome stops being a vague aim and becomes a target you can work toward. If you know the verb, the subject and what counts as evidence, you know what your work has to do.
