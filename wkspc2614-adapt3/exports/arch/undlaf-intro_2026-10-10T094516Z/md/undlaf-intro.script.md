## Course introduction  <!-- page understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-OPEN-co-01 -->

#### Overview and justification  <!-- understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-OPEN-c-05 text -->

Three things shape how you get on at university: how you learn, how you are assessed, and the feedback you get along the way. Some students meet these one at a time and treat them as separate hurdles. This course proposes a different view. It shows how learning, assessment and feedback work together as one cycle, one you will meet again and again throughout your studies. Understanding how these parts of academic studies connect is worth your attention right at the start, because it lets you study with purpose from the outset rather than spend your first terms working it out the hard way.

Over five short lessons you will look at what learning at university actually asks of you, and at how university spells out what you are meant to learn. You will then look at what assessment is really for, at how to make feedback worth having, and finally at how the three connect into a single cycle you can run deliberately. None of it is abstract: it all applies to the work you are already doing, this term, in whatever you are studying. By the end you will have a clearer picture of how your own studies work, and a few practical habits for making each piece of work feed the next.

#### Course learning outcomes  <!-- understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-OPEN-c-10 text -->

By the end of this course you will be able to:

- apply an understanding of how university learning works to your own study;

- interpret your own assessment tasks and marking criteria;

- act on feedback to improve your own work;

- align your own academic practice with the learning, assessment and feedback cycle.

These matter because none of them belongs to a single module: every subject you take will set work, mark it against criteria, and give you feedback. The sooner you understand how that cycle works, the more each piece of work you do can build on the last.

#### Academic and professional practice  <!-- understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-OPEN-c-15 text -->

Everything in this course applies to the work you are already doing. Every module you take sets assessments, marks them against criteria, and gives you feedback; the students who understand that cycle, and run it on purpose, are the ones whose work improves from one assignment to the next rather than repeating the same mistakes. Treat what follows as being about your own essays, reports and exams, not about study in the abstract.

The same brief, standard, feedback and next-task cycle runs in many areas of professional practice. You are given a brief, your work is judged against a standard, you receive feedback, and you carry it into the next task. Learning from that feedback and iterating your practice on the back of it is a habit you keep long after you graduate.

### Understanding learning at university  <!-- article L01-a-02 -->

#### Concept: definition  <!-- L01-c-15 text -->

A discourse community is a group of people who share a way of making and testing knowledge: how they ask questions, what counts as good evidence, and how they reach agreement. Every subject at university is its own discourse community, with its own habits of argument.

Learning here is less about collecting facts and more about joining that community. You learn how your subject thinks, not only what it has concluded. This matters because it changes what getting better means: progress is not storing more information, but taking a fuller part in how your subject builds knowledge.

### Understanding learning outcomes  <!-- article L02-a-02 -->

#### Concept: definition  <!-- L02-c-15 text -->

A learning outcome is a statement of what you should be able to do by the end of a module. It describes what you will be able to demonstrate, not just what will be covered in class. Every module is built around a set of these, usually printed in the module handbook or on its page in your virtual learning environment.

They matter because your assessment is, in effect, asking you to evidence and demonstrate them. The outcome tells you what you are aiming at; the assessment is where you show you have reached it. Learning to read an outcome closely is one of the most useful habits you can build early, because it tells you what success actually looks like.

#### Concept: in practice  <!-- L02-c-20 text -->

#### Inside a learning outcome  <!-- L02-c-25 accordion -->

_Select each part to open it. Work through all of them._

A learning outcome is short, but it has parts. Open each one to see what it is doing.

- **Item 1:** The active verb

Every outcome turns on a verb: analyse, evaluate, describe, apply. The verb tells you the kind of thinking you must show, and it is usually the first thing to find.

- **Item 2:** What it is about

After the verb comes the subject of the outcome: the material, skill or situation the verb acts on. This tells you where to point your effort.

- **Item 3:** How you demonstrate it

An outcome is only met when you show it. Ask what would count as evidence: a judgement reached, a method applied, a case explained. This is what your assessment will look for.

- **Item 4:** Why it matters

Read this way, an outcome stops being a vague aim and becomes a target you can work toward. If you know the verb, the subject and what counts as evidence, you know what your work has to do.

#### Worked example  <!-- L02-c-30 text -->

Two students read the same learning outcome from their module handbook: Evaluate the usefulness of a common method in your field.

{{learner-a}} reads it quickly and starts describing the method: what it is, how it works, where it is used. The writing is accurate, but it only describes. The outcome asked for something the writing never does.

{{learner-b}} reads the same outcome and breaks it down first. The verb is evaluate, which means weighing strengths against weaknesses to reach a judgement. What it is about is the method's usefulness, not simply how it works. ### Understanding assessment at university  <!-- article L03-a-02 -->

#### Concept: definition  <!-- L03-c-15 text -->

Assessment is how your work is judged against the standards of your subject. At university it can be broad in approach and takes many forms. In one way or another it is almost always present, because it is how your tutors see whether you have met a module's learning outcomes.

Reading an assessment well, knowing what kind of task it is and what it is for, lets you give it the right kind of effort rather than guessing. That starts with seeing how varied assessment is, and with one distinction that changes how much a task counts.

#### Concept: in practice  <!-- L03-c-20 text -->

_⟦ CXP slot L03/concept-context — subject-filled at merge; empty in the base ⟧_

#### The forms assessment takes  <!-- L03-c-25 flipcard -->

_Select each card to turn it over. Work through all six._

Assessment comes in many forms. Turn each card to see what it is, what it asks of you, and the skills that help you do it well.

- **Card 1 front:** Essay

- **Card 1 back:** A written argument in response to a question or title. You take a position and support it with evidence and reasoning, in your subject’s style. To do well you need to build a clear line of argument, select and weigh evidence, and structure your writing so a reader can follow the reasoning. The essay is common because it makes your thinking visible: a marker follows how you reach a conclusion, not only the conclusion itself. That is why a strong essay reads as one connected argument rather than a set of separate points.

- **Card 2 front:** Presentation

- **Card 2 back:** You explain or argue something aloud, often with slides, to tutors or peers. It assesses how clearly you can communicate ideas, not only hold them. Succeeding draws on organising material for a listener, speaking clearly to time, and using visuals that support rather than crowd what you say. It is set because so much academic and professional work depends on being understood by others, not only on being right. The clearer you make a complex idea for your audience, the more you show you have understood it yourself.

- **Card 3 front:** Portfolio

- **Card 3 back:** A collection of work gathered over time, sometimes with a reflective commentary. It shows how you have developed, not just a single finished piece. It rewards steady organisation, honest self-review, and the ability to show progress by selecting and explaining the right pieces. A portfolio is used when how you got there matters as much as the final result, so it rewards work built steadily rather than assembled at the last minute. Choosing what to include, and saying why, is itself part of what is assessed.

- **Card 4 front:** Reflective piece

- **Card 4 back:** Writing in which you examine your own learning: what you did, what you noticed, and what you would change. The thinking is the point. Doing it well means looking honestly at your own practice, connecting experience to ideas from your subject, and drawing out what you will do differently. It is assessed because learning from experience is a skill in its own right, one you will use throughout your studies and your working life. What earns credit is honest, specific insight into your own practice, not a tidy account that avoids any difficulty.

- **Card 5 front:** Exam

- **Card 5 back:** A timed assessment under set conditions. It tests what you can recall and do without your notes to hand. Success rests on secure recall, planning an answer quickly under time pressure, and applying what you know to the question actually asked. Exams test what you can do independently, with no support to hand, which is why they reward secure understanding over last-minute memorising. Practising under timed conditions beforehand is what turns what you know into what you can show on the day.

- **Card 6 front:** Practice-based assessment

- **Card 6 back:** An assessment of what you can do in practice: a laboratory or studio task, a clinical or professional placement, a performance, or fieldwork. It judges applied skill and professional judgement in a real or realistic setting, not only what you can write about it. To do well you need to apply knowledge to the task in front of you, work safely and to your field’s standards, and show sound judgement as conditions change.

#### Worked example  <!-- L03-c-30 text -->

Two students are given the same draft essay to hand in for comments. It is a formative task: the tutor will mark up the draft, but the grade will not count. The graded essay comes later.

{{learner-a}} sees that the draft does not count and puts little into it, handing in a rough attempt and barely reading the comments that come back. When the graded essay is due, {{learner-a}} makes the same mistakes the comments had already flagged.

{{learner-b}} treats the draft as a chance to find out what is not working yet. The comments point out a weak line of argument, so {{learner-b}} reworks it before the graded essay. The final piece is stronger, because the formative task did its job.

Both students did the same formative task. Only {{learner-b}} used what it is for: formative assessment is there to help you improve before the summative assessment that sets your grade.

### Understanding the learning, assessment and feedback cycle  <!-- article L05-a-02 -->

#### Concept: definition  <!-- L05-c-15 text -->

It is easy to treat learning, assessment and feedback as three separate things: you learn in lectures and reading, you are assessed in essays and exams, and feedback turns up afterwards. Dealt with one at a time, each can feel like a hurdle to clear and then forget.

They work far better understood as one connected cycle, where each part feeds the next and the whole thing comes round again. Understanding that connection is what this lesson builds, because it is the difference between repeating the same cycle and improving with each turn of it. In a moment you will map out how the parts follow on from one another; first, it is worth seeing why thinking of them as a loop changes how you study.

## Course conclusion  <!-- page understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-CLOSE-co-01 -->

#### Summary  <!-- understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-CLOSE-c-05 text -->

You have reached the end of the course. You now have more than a set of facts about university study: you have a way of working strategically. You can take a deliberate, strategic approach to developing your own learning and academic practice throughout your studies, rather than meeting each module, assessment and piece of feedback as it comes. In particular, you can:

- apply an understanding of how university learning works to your own study: you have seen what learning at university asks of you, and how modules set out what you are meant to learn;

- interpret your own assessment tasks and marking criteria: you can read what a task is really asking of you;

- act on feedback to improve your own work: you can work out the action behind a comment and carry it into your next piece;

- align your own academic practice with the learning, assessment and feedback cycle: you have seen how the three connect, and how to run them as one cycle rather than three separate steps.

You have just rated your confidence again on the previous page. Comparing it with the check you took at the start is your own measure of how far you have come.

#### Academic and professional practice  <!-- understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-CLOSE-c-10 text -->

You now have a way of seeing your own studies that will serve you in every module from here on. Each assessment you meet is a chance to show what you have learned, each piece of feedback a pointer to what to do next, and the two together are how your work improves across the degree. The habit to keep is simple: read what each task is asking, use the feedback you get, and let it shape the next piece of work.

That same habit carries into professional life. Wherever you are given work, judged against a standard, and told how to improve, the people who get better are the ones who treat each round as feeding the next. The cycle you have just learned to see is one you will use long after you graduate.

#### Where this goes next  <!-- understanding-learning-assessment-feedback-introductory-W-CLOSE-c-15 text -->

This is an introductory course: it gives you the shape of how learning, assessment and feedback work together. You can take it further from here.

Go deeper on this topic. The Intermediate and Advanced tiers of Understanding Learning, Assessment and Feedback take the same cycle further, into harder cases and more of your own judgement.

Put it into practice. Other pathways in this programme apply what you have just learned. Academic Writing is where most of your assessed work is produced and most feedback lands. Criticality develops the kind of thinking that higher-level assessment rewards. Reflective Practice turns on a similar iterative cycle, where you act, reflect on how it went, and carry what you learn into your next attempt, so the habit you have built here transfers straight across.
