### Course outcomes (`crs-03_course-outcomes`)

1. **co1**: Apply an understanding of how university learning works to your own study.
2. **co2**: Interpret your own assessment tasks and marking criteria.
3. **co3**: Act on feedback to improve your own work.
4. **co4**: Align your own academic practice with the learning, assessment and feedback cycle.

**courseOutcomeCountReason**

Four, not three: co1-co3 are the component themes (learning, assessment, feedback); co4 is integrative. The course's strategic point is that learning, assessment and feedback do not operate in isolation but as a connected cycle the learner aligns their own practice with. That synthesis is distinct from the three component outcomes and cannot be folded into any one of them.

**academic**

<p>Everything in this course applies to the work you are already doing. Every module you take sets assessments, marks them against criteria, and gives you feedback; the students who understand that cycle, and run it on purpose, are the ones whose work improves from one assignment to the next rather than repeating the same mistakes. Treat what follows as being about your own essays, reports and exams, not about study in the abstract.</p>


**professional**

<p>The same brief, standard, feedback and next-task cycle runs in many areas of professional practice. You are given a brief, your work is judged against a standard, you receive feedback, and you carry it into the next task. Learning from that feedback and iterating your practice on the back of it is a habit you keep long after you graduate.</p>


**overview**

<p>Three things shape how you get on at university: how you learn, how you are assessed, and the feedback you get along the way. Most students meet these one at a time and treat them as separate hurdles. This course takes a different view. It shows how learning, assessment and feedback work together as one cycle, and why seeing that connection is what lets you study with purpose rather than just keep up.</p><p>Over five short lessons you will look at what learning at university actually asks of you, and at how university spells out what you are meant to learn. You will then look at what assessment is really for, at how to make feedback worth having, and finally at how the three connect into a single cycle you can run deliberately. None of it is abstract: it all applies to the work you are already doing, this term, in whatever you are studying. By the end you will have a clearer picture of how your own studies work, and a few practical habits for making each piece of work feed the next.</p>


**courseOutcomesFraming**

<p>By the end of this course you will be able to:</p><ul><li>apply an understanding of how university learning works to your own study;</li><li>interpret your own assessment tasks and marking criteria;</li><li>act on feedback to improve your own work;</li><li>align your own academic practice with the learning, assessment and feedback cycle.</li></ul><p>These matter because none of them belongs to a single module: every subject you take will set work, mark it against criteria, and give you feedback. The sooner you understand how that cycle works, the more each piece of work you do can build on the last.</p>

**academic**

<p>You now have a way of seeing your own studies that will serve you in every module from here on. Each assessment you meet is a chance to show what you have learned, each piece of feedback a pointer to what to do next, and the two together are how your work improves across the degree. The habit to keep is simple: read what each task is asking, use the feedback you get, and let it shape the next piece of work.</p>


**professional**

<p>That same habit carries into professional life. Wherever you are given work, judged against a standard, and told how to improve, the people who get better are the ones who treat each round as feeding the next. The cycle you have just learned to see is one you will use long after you graduate.</p>


**summary**

<p>You have reached the end of the course. You now have more than a set of facts about university study: you have a way of working strategically. You can take a deliberate, strategic approach to developing your own learning and academic practice throughout your studies, rather than meeting each module, assessment and piece of feedback as it comes. In particular, you can:</p><ul><li>apply an understanding of how university learning works to your own study: you have seen what learning at university asks of you, and how modules set out what you are meant to learn;</li><li>interpret your own assessment tasks and marking criteria: you can read what a task is really asking of you;</li><li>act on feedback to improve your own work: you can work out the action behind a comment and carry it into your next piece;</li><li>align your own academic practice with the learning, assessment and feedback cycle: you have seen how the three connect, and how to run them as one cycle rather than three separate steps.</li></ul><p>You have just rated your confidence again on the previous page. Comparing it with the check you took at the start is your own measure of how far you have come.</p>

Academic Writing is where most of your assessed work is produced and most feedback lands. Criticality develops the kind of thinking that higher-level assessment rewards. Reflective Practice turns on a similar iterative cycle, where you act, reflect on how it went, and carry what you learn into your next attempt, so the habit you have built here transfers straight across.</p>
