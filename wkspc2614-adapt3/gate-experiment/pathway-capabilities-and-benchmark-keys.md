Generated by `tools/render-pathway-benchmarks-md.py`; do not hand-edit. Re-run after `tools/key-benchmark-tiers.py` or after editing `catalogue/context/pathway-evidence/`.

- **The 9:** each pathway's capabilities, 3 per tier (`catalogue/context/pathway/<pathway>.json`).
- **The 18:** two evidence examples per capability, one **conceptual** (theory, concept or model) and one **applied** (practice, real world): 6 per tier (`catalogue/context/pathway-evidence/<pathway>.json`). Draft for review.
- **Attribution:** whether a benchmark lands on the capability. **direct** = a direct benchmark matched it; **indirect only**; **UNATTRIBUTED** = no benchmark matched it (every-tier keys do not count).
- **Benchmark keys:** universal graduate competencies and subject statements (Psychology, the one authored subject map, keyed as a draft) that key into the pathway at each tier.

Tier basis: **match** = matched a capability at that tier; **every tier** = progression (direct, no match) or floor (indirect, no match: weakest, review these).

**The 18 at this tier (evidence a student could offer):**

1. **Explain what excelling at university means**
    - *Conceptual: What excelling means, explained through deep and surface approaches.* You read a short account of deep and surface approaches to learning (Marton and Saljo 1976) and use it to write a paragraph on what excelling at university means: seeking meaning and connecting ideas, not only collecting marks or memorising. You add one example of each approach from your own first weeks. This shows you can explain what excelling involves (for example, a foundation-year design student contrasting copying a tutor's sketch style with understanding why it works). **Evidence:** A short written explanation of excelling at university, using deep and surface approaches, with two examples from your own study. *(Grounding: model: Deep and surface approaches to learning (Marton and Saljo 1976))*
    - *Applied: Interview with an experienced student about doing well.* You interview a second- or third-year student, a student ambassador or your personal tutor about what doing well at university looked like for them, then write a short summary comparing their answer with what you expected from school or college. This shows you can explain what excelling at university means in practice (for example, a criminology student learning that excelling meant reading beyond the list and speaking in seminars, not only grades). **Evidence:** Interview notes and a half-page summary comparing your expectations of university with what an experienced student told you. *(Grounding: practice: conversation with a student ambassador or personal tutor)*
2. **Recognise and follow the conventions of academic practice**
    - *Conceptual: Own writing annotated as disciplinary practice.* You annotate one piece of your writing against your discipline's conventions (referencing style, structure, tone, use of evidence), then explain, using the academic literacies model (Lea and Street 1998), why these are practices of a particular discipline rather than general study skills learned once. This shows you recognising and following the conventions of academic practice (for example, a chemistry student noticing that lab reports use set sections and an impersonal style, unlike an English essay). **Evidence:** An annotated piece of your writing marking each convention followed, with a short note on what your discipline expects. *(Grounding: model: Academic literacies (Lea and Street 1998))*
    - *Applied: Real document written to an organisation's house style.* You find the conventions expected in a setting outside your course, such as a society, volunteering role or part-time job, and compare them with your discipline's. You then follow them in a real task, like writing minutes or a client email. This shows you can recognise and follow conventions when joining a community (for example, a politics student writing their first press release for the students' union in its house style). **Evidence:** Your finished document beside the house style guide or template, with a note on the conventions you followed. *(Grounding: professional: Learns and follows the conventions of a profession or organisation on joining it.)*
3. **Adopt a growth mindset towards academic challenge**
    - *Conceptual: A setback rewritten in fixed and growth mindset terms.* You take a piece of work that went less well than you hoped and write two short accounts of it, one in fixed-mindset terms and one in growth-mindset terms, drawing on Dweck's (2006) distinction between believing ability is fixed and believing it grows with effort and good strategy. You then set one effort-based next step. This shows you adopting a growth mindset towards academic challenge (for example, a mathematics student reframing a low first-term problem sheet mark). **Evidence:** Two contrasting reflections on one setback, fixed and growth framed, ending with a concrete next step you then took. *(Grounding: model: Mindset theory (Dweck 2006))*
    - *Applied: A stretch challenge taken on with feedback sought midway.* You take on a challenge you would usually avoid, such as a hard optional module, a coding competition or a presentation for your society, and ask for feedback partway through. You record what you did with that feedback. This shows you treating difficult work as a chance to learn (for example, a music student entering a composition competition and asking a tutor for comments on a draft score). **Evidence:** A record of the challenge, the feedback you asked for and received, and what you changed as a result. *(Grounding: professional: Treats new and difficult work as a chance to learn and seeks feedback to improve.; professional: Vitae RDF B1: personal qualities)*

5. **Build self-efficacy and manage stress and anxiety under pressure**
    - *Conceptual: Confidence plan built on four sources of self-efficacy.* You plan how to build confidence for an assessment you find daunting, using Bandura's (1977) four sources of self-efficacy: mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological and emotional states. This shows you building self-efficacy and managing stress under pressure (for example, an education student preparing for a first micro-teaching session by rehearsing short sections, watching peers and calming nerves beforehand). **Evidence:** A four-source self-efficacy plan with the actions you took and a short evaluation of which helped most. *(Grounding: model: Sources of self-efficacy (Bandura 1977))*

This shows you managing stress under real pressure (for example, a journalism student meeting deadlines on the student newspaper during exam season). **Evidence:** A dated stress and coping log across the pressured period, with a short end-of-period review. *(Grounding: professional: Keeps confidence and performance under pressure and copes with stress.; professional: Tomlinson (2017): psychological capital)*
6. **Develop an academic voice and the confidence to own one's practice**

This shows the confidence to own your practice in your own voice (for example, an anthropology student presenting fieldwork findings at a student conference and answering challenge from staff). **Evidence:** Your abstract or slides, the questions you were asked and a short reflection on how you responded. *(Grounding: professional: Speaks and writes with a professional voice and owns their practice within the profession's standards.)*

## Understanding Learning, Assessment and Feedback

**The 18 at this tier (evidence a student could offer):**

1. **Understand how learning works in a discipline's discourse community**
    - *Conceptual: Map of your discipline as a community of practice.* You map your discipline as a community using Lave and Wenger's (1991) idea of legitimate peripheral participation: newcomers learn by taking part in real but limited ways and move towards full participation. You mark where you are now as a newcomer and the activities, such as seminars, reading and writing, that move you inward. This shows you understand how learning works in your discipline's discourse community (for example, a first-year archaeology student placing themselves relative to lecturers who excavate and publish). **Evidence:** A labelled diagram of your discipline's community showing your position and the activities that move you towards fuller participation. *(Grounding: ing:doc:situated-learning-lave-wenger-1991)*
    - *Applied: Notes from a research seminar on how the discipline argues.* You attend a real event in your discipline's community, such as a departmental research seminar, a guest lecture or a learned society meeting, and note how people talk, argue and use evidence there. You then write a short account of what this taught you about how the discipline learns. This shows you understanding a discourse community from the inside (for example, a physics student noting how a speaker's results were questioned at a colloquium). **Evidence:** Event notes and a short account of the community's ways of talking, arguing and using evidence. *(Grounding: professional: Learns how a profession or organisation works, its norms, people and ways of knowing, on joining it.)*
2. **Tell formative from summative assessment and engage with varied forms**
    - *Conceptual: Module assessments sorted and aligned to outcomes.* You list the assessments in one module, label each formative or summative, and use Biggs' (1996) constructive alignment to show which learning outcome each is designed to check. You then note how you will use the formative tasks to prepare for the summative ones. This shows you can tell the two apart and engage with varied forms (for example, a first-year marketing student seeing that a practice pitch prepares for the assessed group presentation). **Evidence:** A module assessment table labelled formative or summative, each linked to an outcome, with your plan for using the formative tasks. *(Grounding: ing:doc:constructive-alignment-biggs-1996)*
    - *Applied: Optional formative draft used to improve a marked piece.* You take part in a formative task you could skip, such as a draft submission, a practice quiz or a peer review, and use the feedback to change the final piece before it is marked. This shows you engaging with varied assessment forms and treating rehearsal as a chance to improve (for example, a film studies student submitting an essay plan for comment and restructuring the argument as a result). **Evidence:** Your formative draft with its feedback, beside the summative submission showing the changes you made. *(Grounding: professional: Uses practice runs, drafts and reviews as chances to improve before work is judged.)*
3. **Tell active, constructivist learning from passive reception**
    - *Conceptual: Two study sessions compared as deep and surface approaches.* You record two study sessions on the same reading, one spent rereading and highlighting and one spent questioning, summarising in your own words and linking to earlier topics. Using Marton and Saljo's (1976) distinction between deep and surface approaches, you explain which session was active and constructivist and what you could explain afterwards. This shows you can tell active learning from passive reception (for example, a philosophy student comparing their grasp of a Kant extract after each session). **Evidence:** Notes from both sessions side by side, with a short commentary classifying each approach and what you could explain afterwards. *(Grounding: ing:doc:approaches-to-learning-marton-saljo-1976)*
    - *Applied: Learning a work task actively instead of waiting to be shown.* In a job, volunteering or society role, you notice a point where you were waiting to be told what to do and instead learn it actively: asking questions, trying it and checking your understanding. You write up the contrast. This shows you distinguishing active from passive learning in practice (for example, a computing student in a summer IT support job learning a ticketing system by working through real requests rather than reading the manual). **Evidence:** A short before-and-after account of learning a task at work, confirmed by a note from your supervisor. *(Grounding: professional: Takes an active part in own learning at work rather than waiting to be trained.; professional: Vitae RDF B2: self-management)*

    - *Conceptual: Alignment grid tracing outcomes to activities and assessment.* You take the intended learning outcomes for a module, pick out the verb in each, and trace how lectures, seminars and the assessment are aligned to them, using Biggs' (1996) constructive alignment. Where an outcome has no clear matching activity, you note how you will practise it yourself.     - *Applied: Everyday tasks mapped to role objectives with a supervisor.* In a job, placement or society role, you take the objectives or performance standards you are given, work out how your everyday tasks contribute to each, and discuss the mapping with your supervisor. This shows you reading objectives and seeing how tasks relate to them, as you do with learning outcomes (for example, a hospitality management student mapping hotel reception duties to the guest satisfaction targets in their appraisal). **Evidence:** A mapping of your tasks to your role objectives, annotated after discussion with your supervisor. *(Grounding: professional: Reads objectives and performance standards and sees how tasks and appraisal relate to them.)*
5. **Turn feedback into an action plan through reflection**
    - *Conceptual: Feedback turned into an action plan by reflection-on-action.* You take feedback on a marked assignment and use Schon's (1983) reflection-on-action to look back at the decisions you made while writing it, asking why you made them and what the feedback suggests instead. You turn this into an action plan with specific, dated changes for the next assignment. This shows you using reflective practice to turn feedback into improvement (for example, a social work student reviewing comments on how they applied legislation in a case study). **Evidence:** Annotated feedback, a reflection on your decisions and an action plan of specific, dated changes. *(Grounding: ing:doc:reflective-practitioner-schon-1983)*
    - *Applied: Supervisor feedback acted on through a short development plan.* You turn appraisal or supervisor feedback from a job, placement or volunteering role into a short development plan, act on it over several weeks and review the outcome with the person who gave the feedback. This shows you using feedback to plan improvement beyond the classroom (for example, a retail management student acting on a shift manager's comments about handling customer complaints). **Evidence:** Feedback notes, your development plan and a follow-up review signed or emailed by your supervisor. *(Grounding: professional: Acts on appraisal and colleague feedback through a development plan.; professional: Vitae RDF B3: professional and career development)*
6. **Apply self-regulated learning cycles to independent study**
    - *Conceptual: Two documented cycles of self-regulated independent study.* You plan, monitor and review a block of independent study using Zimmerman's (2002) cyclical model of self-regulated learning: forethought (setting goals and strategies), performance (monitoring as you work) and self-reflection (evaluating results). You complete at least two full cycles and show how the second changed in light of the first. This shows you applying self-regulated learning to independent study (for example, a modern languages student preparing for an oral exam through weekly practice cycles). **Evidence:** Two completed cycle records, covering forethought, performance and self-reflection, showing what changed between the first and second. *(Grounding: model: Cyclical model of self-regulated learning (Zimmerman 2002))*
    - *Applied: Progress log kept across a live client brief.* During a long independent project, such as a dissertation, a self-directed lab project or a live client brief, you set weekly goals, keep a progress log and adjust your approach at set review points, sharing the log with your supervisor. This shows you applying self-regulated cycles to real independent work (for example, a product design student running a twelve-week brief for a local charity). **Evidence:** A project progress log with weekly goals, monitoring notes and recorded adjustments, shared with your supervisor. *(Grounding: professional: Plans own work and development, monitors progress against goals and adjusts approach.; professional: Vitae RDF B2: self-management)*
