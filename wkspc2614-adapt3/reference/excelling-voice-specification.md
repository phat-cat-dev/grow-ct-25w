# Excelling Voice: Content Generation Specification
## Academic Skills Module: Voice, Style, and Register Rules

**Version:** v1.1, September 2026
**Status:** Authoritative single source; all other documents reference this file
**Derived from:** Linguistic audit of Dennis, C. and Abbott, S. (2026) *Excelling at University*. Routledge. Merged with project conventions established during the Academic Writing pilot.
**Note:** The machine-readable JSON version (`generation/voice-rules.json`) is superseded by this file. This markdown is the single source of truth.

---

## 1. Source and standard

All student-facing content takes its voice and approach from *Excelling at University* (Dennis & Abbott, 2026). Content must be consistent with its register, its frameworks, and its positions. Where a topic falls outside the book's scope, maintain its voice and pedagogical approach.

**The quality test:** Could this sentence have appeared in that book? If the answer is no, diagnose why and revise.

---

## 2. Core pedagogical principle

**Explain the *why*, not just the *what* or the *how*.** This is the source text's central distinguishing principle and therefore this project's central voice principle. Content does not merely tell students what to do; it explains the rationale: why tutors expect it, what the skill means for their development, why it matters.

**Rule:** Every significant piece of advice in lesson copy must carry its reason. If a sentence tells the student to do something without explaining why, add the why or restructure.

**Good:** "Tutors expect you to acknowledge your sources because it shows you understand that knowledge in your field is built collectively; each piece of work sits within a wider academic conversation."

**Not good enough:** "You must reference all sources used in your work."

---

## 3. Language and spelling

British English throughout. -ise (not -ize), -our (not -or), -re (not -er). Key variants: organised, recognised, behaviour, colour, centre, practise (verb) / practice (noun), licence (noun) / license (verb), programme (not program, except in computing contexts).

Spell out one to nine; use digits for 10 and above. Use digits for all measurements, percentages, and page references. Use en dashes for ranges (e.g. '45–60 minutes', 'pp. 12–15').

---

## 4. Punctuation

**Em dashes:** Never used. Use commas, semicolons, colons, or parentheses instead. This is a project-wide rule.

**En dashes:** Ranges only (number ranges, page ranges). Not sentence punctuation.

**Semicolons:** Used freely to join related independent clauses. Approximately 1–2 per page. A distinctive feature of the register.

**Colons:** Introduce lists, explanations, elaborations. Before block quotes and worked examples.

**Parentheses:** Brief asides, inline definitions, in-text references. 1–3 per paragraph at most.

**Commas:** No Oxford comma. Be consistent throughout.

**Quotation marks:** Single quotes for terms being defined, discussed, or problematised (e.g. 'study skills', 'being critical'). Double quotes for direct speech and direct quotations from sources. Punctuation inside quotation marks only where it belongs to the quoted material.

---

## 5. Formatting

**Bold:** Headings, task labels, UI element labels only. Never for emphasis in running prose.

**Italics:** Emphasis (sparingly), book titles, key terms on first introduction, Latin terms.

**Bullet points:** Explicit list contexts only (checklists, task instructions, learning outcomes). Never in running prose. A three-bullet list is not a substitute for a two-sentence explanation that connects the ideas.

**Numbered lists:** Marking criteria, principles, step-by-step guidance, ordered sequences.

**Block quotes:** Extended writing exemplars and student scenario narratives. Typically italicised.

**Headings:** Hierarchical, active or topic-focused phrasing. Not questions (except occasionally). Not numbered.

---

## 6. Address and person

**Student address:** Always 'you' and 'your' in explanatory prose. Never 'students should', 'the learner', 'one might'. Third-person limited to referring to other students in examples or scenarios.

**Implied author:** A knowledgeable colleague or peer. Not an institution, not a lecturer, not an authority figure. The voice knows its subject and speaks from experience but never talks down. Treats the reader as capable of understanding the full picture if it is explained clearly.

**'We' usage:** Acceptable for shared assumptions ('As we saw in Topic 3...') but not the dominant pattern. Use sparingly.

**'They' usage:** Singular gender-neutral pronoun where needed. Avoid 'he or she'.

---

## 7. Tone and register

**Overall register:** Mid-register. More formal than conversational English, less formal than an academic journal article. Same register band as quality broadsheet features or well-written professional non-fiction.

**Contractions:** Used freely in explanatory prose and direct student address. Standard set: it's, you're, we're, they're, don't, doesn't, won't, can't, that's, there's, we've, you've, you'll, isn't, aren't, we'll. Avoid: ain't, gonna, wanna, could've. **Exception:** Do not use contractions in formal definitions, learning outcomes, or assessment feedback copy.

**Tone spectrum:** The voice operates on a spectrum within a single register, not distinct modes:

- *Conversational end:* Light humour, everyday analogies, acknowledgement of student feelings. Used at paragraph openings and transitional moments.
- *Instructional middle (default):* Clear, direct explanation with practical guidance.
- *Analytical end:* Worked example analysis and marking criteria discussion.

**Warmth without patronising:** Acknowledge challenges genuinely without being condescending. Name the feeling, then move to practical guidance. Never pretend things are easy when they are not.

**Honesty about difficulty:** The source text does not pretend things are easy. It acknowledges when a skill takes time, when students commonly struggle, and when effort is required. Lesson copy does the same.

**Good:** "Planning an argument before you write can feel frustrating; it's tempting to just start. But the students who plan consistently produce stronger work, because they know what they're trying to say before they say it."

**Not good enough:** "Planning is simple. Just follow these steps."

## 8. Sentence-level conventions

### 8.1 Length and rhythm

Average 18–22 words per sentence. Vary length with a distinctive rhythm: medium sentences (15–25 words) form the backbone, punctuated by short declarative sentences (5–12 words) for emphasis and occasional longer sentences (30–45 words) for qualification.

**Typical rhythm:** Medium → medium → short (emphasis) → long (qualification) → medium.

Short sentences carry the key takeaway or mark a shift in direction. Use deliberately. After a longer explanation, landing a key point in a very short sentence is a signature move of the source text.

Never exceed approximately 45 words. Never use three or more long sentences consecutively. If three consecutive sentences are a similar length, vary the fourth.

### 8.2 Openers

Vary sentence openers within each paragraph. No two consecutive sentences should begin with the same word or grammatical structure.

Permitted types: subject-verb, conditional (If...), prepositional (In..., At...), temporal (When..., After...), contrast (However..., That said...), demonstrative (This...), imperative (Think..., Consider...), participial (Having...).

Avoid starting more than one sentence per paragraph with 'This' or 'It'. Avoid 'There is/are' where a more direct construction is available.

### 8.3 Active and passive voice

Approximately 75% active, 25% passive. Default to active for all instructional and practical content. Use passive when: the concept is more important than the actor; describing conventions or norms; the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant.

**Good:** "Tutors expect clearly structured essays."

**Avoid:** "Essays are expected to be structured clearly."

### 8.4 Nominalisations

Avoid where a verb would be clearer. 'Analyse' not 'conduct an analysis of'. 'Decide' not 'make a decision about'. 'Evaluate' not 'carry out an evaluation of'.

---

## 9. Hedging

**Principle:** Hedge selectively, not reflexively. The default for instructional content is confident. Reserve hedging for genuinely uncertain or variable claims.

**Hedge when:** describing individual student experience; stating variable outcomes; acknowledging contested positions; qualifying claims about student populations.

**Be confident when:** describing established academic conventions; defining marking criteria; explaining skill descriptions; stating what a piece of writing demonstrates.

**Hedging devices:** broadly speaking, generally, often, in most cases, tends to, is likely to, may, might, can, could, perhaps, in our experience, it's worth noting that, it's fair to say that.

**Anti-pattern (imprecise):** Avoid hedges that weaken without adding honesty: 'may or may not', 'in some cases it could be argued', 'some people think'. Hedge precisely or do not hedge.

**Anti-pattern (double hedge):** Never hedge then immediately undermine the hedge ('This might be important, but it's actually essential'). If the claim is established, state it confidently.

---

## 10. Paragraph-level conventions

**Length:** 3–5 sentences typical. 2–7 permitted. Single-sentence paragraphs extremely rare.

**Structure:** Topic sentence → development → implication. Every paragraph has a discernible topic sentence (usually first). The final sentence connects back to the student's practice or signals the next point. Avoid paragraphs that are purely descriptive with no implication for the student.

**Transitions:** Embed the transition in the first sentence of the new paragraph. Use a substantive transition that connects the ideas rather than merely announcing a topic shift.

**Preferred transitions:** 'That said, ...', 'In addition to X, ...', 'Beyond X, ...', 'Let's take a closer look at...', 'So much for X; how do you...'

**Avoid as paragraph openers:** 'Furthermore, ...', 'Moreover, ...', 'Additionally, ...', 'In conclusion, ...', 'Moving on to...', 'Now let's look at...', 'Last but not least...', 'To summarise the above...'

---

## 11. Rhetorical strategies

### 11.1 Inclusive acknowledgement

Before introducing a new skill or expectation, acknowledge why it might feel unfamiliar or challenging. Be honest about difficulty. Do this in one sentence, not an extended empathy paragraph. Then move directly into practical guidance.

### 11.2 Reframing

A signature move: surface the common misconception, then pivot to the richer understanding. State the misconception briefly and without judgement, then pivot using 'but', 'yet', or 'however'. Avoid 'actually' (can sound condescending).

**Example:** "Students usually see referencing in the context of plagiarism... Yet referencing is about much more than avoiding plagiarism."

### 11.3 Concrete before abstract

Ground concepts in a specific example, scenario, or case study first. Generalise after. Do not start with a definition and then illustrate. Start with the illustration and extract the principle. AI will default to definition-first unless explicitly instructed otherwise.

### 11.4 Question then answer

Pose a question the student is likely to be asking, then answer it immediately. Use to anticipate student questions and maintain engagement. Reserve for moments of genuine ambiguity or student hesitation.

**Example:** "Why should you spend time reading this book? Well, as we've already said, this book stands apart from similar titles..."

### 11.5 Paired contrast

Structure explanations around two student approaches, concepts, or outcomes. Use where it genuinely illuminates a distinction. Name fictional students where appropriate.

### 11.6 Named student scenarios

Welsh first names only, from the name bank (`design-kb/design-kb.name-bank.json`), no surnames. Pair one female and one male name when contrasting two students, alternate across a course which of them takes the weaker approach, and use a different pair in each lesson. Describe academic behaviour, not demographics. When contrasting approaches, present the weaker approach without mockery or judgement. Let the analysis do the work. All references in scenario passages are fictional.

### 11.7 Direct questions to the reader

Use genuine reflective questions answerable from the student's own experience. Never use questions with an implied correct answer. 'What would you change?' is a real question. 'Have you ever thought about how you approach this?' is rhetorical filler.

### 11.8 Why before how

Explain why something matters before telling the student what to do. One sentence is enough.

### 11.9 Signposted structure

Use explicit signposting in longer explanations: 'In the first part...', 'Let's begin by...', 'Next, you need to...'. Keeps the reader oriented.

## 12. Anti-patterns

These patterns appear in generic AI-generated educational content and must not appear in lesson copy.

### 12.1 Filler openers

Never begin a paragraph with: 'It is important to note that...', 'It is worth remembering that...', 'As we can see...', 'In today's academic environment...', 'In this section, we will explore...', 'Let's dive in...', 'Let's unpack...'

### 12.2 Hollow encouragement

Encouragement must be specific. 'You've identified the main issue; now the question is what to do about it' is specific. 'Great work! You're doing brilliantly!', 'Well done for completing this activity!', 'Excellent job!' are hollow and never used.

### 12.3 Fault and accusation language

Avoid language that places blame or fault on the reader. Discuss common challenges impartially without assuming the reader is personally at fault.

**Avoid:** 'None of this is your fault if you haven't been told.' / 'You probably haven't been doing this correctly.'

**Use instead:** 'If you've struggled with this before...' / 'Many students find this challenging...' / 'These conventions become clear once you know what to look for.'

*Note: this corrects a pattern that occasionally appears in the source text itself. The project convention is the stricter standard.*

### 12.4 Transition clichés

Avoid: 'Moving on to...', 'Now let's look at...', 'Last but not least...', 'To summarise the above...', 'Without further ado...'

### 12.5 Lists as substitute for explanation

A three-bullet list is not a substitute for a two-sentence explanation that connects the ideas. In lesson prose sections, prefer prose.

### 12.6 Imprecise hedging

Avoid: 'may or may not', 'in some cases it could be argued', 'some people think'.

## 13. Vocabulary and register

**Register band:** Mid-register. Uses 'a lot' alongside 'considerable'. Uses 'getting to grips with' alongside 'acquiring competencies'. The conversational and technical sit comfortably together.

**Academic vocabulary:** Define technical terms on first use with a conversational parenthetical or trailing clause. e.g. 'something called self-efficacy, which we'll come back to shortly'. After first definition, use freely; glossary tooltips handle reinforcement.

**Plain English:** Where a shorter word conveys the same meaning, use it. 'Use' not 'utilise'. 'Help' not 'facilitate'. 'Show' not 'demonstrate' (unless the technical sense is intended). One main idea per sentence.

**Frequently used:** therefore, in other words, in addition, however, that said, for example, let's, this is because, the point is, in our experience, it's worth, note that, remember, put simply, in what follows, as we've seen.

**Avoided:** henceforth, thus (rare; 'therefore' preferred), notwithstanding, aforementioned, 'the student' (as subject; always 'you'), 'learner' (as noun), 'one' (impersonal pronoun), shall.

---

## 14. Inclusivity and accessibility

**Student diversity:** Students may be traditional school leavers, mature learners with professional experience, career changers, or international students. Use inclusive examples ('whether that's a school essay, a professional report, or something else entirely').

**Language:** Use 'new or less experienced' rather than 'weak' or 'struggling'. Acknowledge different routes without making any seem deficient.

**Named characters:** Welsh first names from the name bank only (see §11.6). Do not describe demographics.

**No background assumptions:** Do not assume educational background, first language, age, or prior experience.

## 15. Content-type-specific voice

### 15.1 Running prose

Full mid-register with varied rhythm. Reframing, concrete-before-abstract, inclusive acknowledgement, why-before-how all apply. Topic → development → implication paragraph structure.

### 15.2 Worked examples and case studies

Third-person narrative, present tense, italicised block quote for the scenario. Analysis follows immediately using the character's name. Evaluative about the work, not the person. When contrasting, present the weaker example first and analyse neutrally.

### 15.3 Reflective prompts (SRL)

1–3 sentences. Genuine questions only. Must connect to the preceding section; a prompt that could appear anywhere is not a good prompt. 'Take a moment to think about...' is acceptable; 'Write a 200-word response...' is too prescriptive. Include 'no right or wrong answers' where appropriate.

### 15.4 Activity instructions

One sentence on what to do. One optional sentence on what to notice. No more.

**Good:** "Read the passage and apply a tag to each phrase that makes the writing academic. You might find more than one way to describe a feature, and that's fine."

**Bad:** "In this activity, you will be reading an academic passage and identifying various features that are characteristic of academic writing by applying tags to them."

### 15.5 Activity feedback

**Correct:** Brief confirmation plus the reason. Never just 'Correct!'. e.g. 'Yes, this is hedging. The phrase "may suggest" signals that the author is not overstating the claim.'

**Incorrect:** Name what was chosen, explain the issue, point to the right direction without giving the answer away. e.g. 'This phrase is formal vocabulary, not hedging. Hedging is about the strength of the claim, so look for phrases that qualify what the author is saying.'

**Never:** 'Incorrect. Try again.' / 'Wrong answer.' / 'That's not right.'

**Partial:** Acknowledge what was right before addressing what was missed.

**Voice principle:** Feedback is evaluative about the work, never judgemental about the student.

**Contractions:** Acceptable but use more sparingly than in running prose.

### 15.6 Technical reference content

More instructional, less conversational. Still uses 'you' and contractions. 'Note that...' / 'Remember that...' for key points. Show the thing, then explain it.

### 15.7 Glossary definitions

Clear, neutral, concise. First sentence self-contained (used in tooltips). No contractions. No circular definitions. No definitions assuming knowledge of other glossary terms.

### 15.8 Learning outcomes

Formal. No contractions. Action verb + object + condition/context. Use Bloom's taxonomy verbs appropriate to the tier. Avoid vague verbs: 'understand', 'know', 'appreciate', 'be aware of'.

### 15.9 Cross-pathway references

Conversational framing. 'This connects to what you explored in Topic 3.' Never formal notation ('See Section 3.2.1'). Frame connected pathways as opportunities, not requirements.

### 15.10 Wrapper overview (practice-level justification)

Distinct from Running prose's lesson-scoped orientation (§15.1): pitched at the whole practice, not one lesson's topic. Must explicitly connect the practice to something beyond this tier (academic progression, other assessments or modules, professional practice after graduation), not just "this matters because you'll use it here." Still mid-register, contractions permitted, why-before-how still applies (§11.8). One additional rule specific to this content type: name at least one concrete, external stake (an assessment type, a module, a professional context) rather than asserting importance in the abstract. Abstraction here reads as filler (§12.1) even though the register itself stays conversational.

**Good:** "Almost everything you submit at university (essays, reports, dissertations) depends on the same underlying skill: turning research into an argument someone else can follow. Get this right once, and it carries into every module that asks you to write."

**Bad:** "Academic writing is an important skill that you will need throughout your degree and beyond."

## 16. Tier calibration

Each tier speaks to an overlapping band of study levels. Content is pitched at the primary level but remains accessible to the full band. All rules above apply to all tiers; the calibration below adjusts within those rules.

| Tier | Primary | Speaks to | Voice summary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Foundation** | L3 | L3, L4 | No prior HE experience assumed. Explain from scratch. Everyday language and analogies. Outcome verbs: identify, describe, recognise, list, name, state. |
| **Beginner** | L4 | L3, L4, L5 | Minimal academic experience assumed. First principles but not over-explained. Confident first step, not remedial. Outcome verbs: describe, identify, recognise, explain, distinguish, connect. |
| **Intermediate** | L5 | L4, L5, L6 | Foundations in place. Build without re-explaining basics. More nuanced examples. Student developing academic identity. Outcome verbs: analyse, compare, apply, evaluate, discuss, interpret. |
| **Advanced** | L6 | L5, L6, L7 | Competence assumed. Sophistication, independence, critical self-awareness. Tone shifts to collegial. Outcome verbs: critique, evaluate, synthesise, construct, justify, interrogate. |
| **Masters** | L7 | L6, L7, L8 | Advanced critical capability. Methodological awareness, scholarly positioning. Transition from consumer to producer of knowledge. Outcome verbs: critically evaluate, synthesise, construct, defend, position, problematise. |
| **Doctoral** | L8 | L8 | Research independence and scholarly expertise. Original contribution, publication standard. Peer-to-peer tone. Outcome verbs: originate, defend, position, contribute, problematise, reconceptualise. |

**Tier-specific adjustments:**

- **Sentence length:** Foundation averages 15–18 words; Beginner/Intermediate 18–22; Advanced 20–28; Masters 22–30; Doctoral uncapped but clarity paramount.
- **Vocabulary:** Foundation defines everything with plain English alternatives. Beginner defines on first use. Intermediate uses terminology freely with brief reminders. Advanced assumes familiarity. Masters and Doctoral use full scholarly register.
- **Examples:** Foundation uses school/work/social media analogies. Beginner uses first assignment/seminar/feedback scenarios. Intermediate uses analysis/comparison tasks. Advanced uses methodological choices and discourse positioning. Masters uses research design and theoretical positioning. Doctoral uses thesis structure, examiner expectations, publication preparation.

## 17. Referencing conventions

**Primary system:** Harvard (Cite Them Right).

**Format:** Dennis, C. and Abbott, S. (2026) *Excelling at University: Academic Literacies and the Development of Graduate Identity*. 1st edn. Routledge. doi:10.4324/9781003217527.

**Exceptions:** OSCOLA for Law. APA for some Psychology contexts. Check module/programme requirements.

**Fictional references:** All references in activity passages and worked examples are fictional. Use plausible author names, years, and publication patterns. Mark as fictional in build notes, not in student-facing copy.

**In-text style:** Author-date. (Walker, 2017) or Walker (2017). Ampersand in parenthetical; 'and' in running text. *et al.* for three or more authors.

**Reference list:** Alphabetical by author surname. Only cited sources. 'References' as the heading.

## 18. The register check

Apply these six questions before finalising any piece of lesson copy:

1. **Does every significant piece of advice carry its reason?** If not, add the why or restructure.
2. **Is the student addressed as 'you' in explanatory prose?** Third-person should be rare.
3. **Is the tone direct without being blunt?** Never condescend. Never wrap advice in so many qualifications that it becomes useless.
4. **Are examples concrete?** If a paragraph explains a concept without a specific instance, consider whether an example would clarify it.
5. **Does the content avoid assumptions about student backgrounds?** Students may be school leavers, mature learners, career changers, or international students.
6. **Could this sentence have appeared in *Excelling at University*?** If not, diagnose why and revise.

---

## Quick reference

*Direct, warm, honest, specific: explaining the why behind every what, addressing the student as a capable adult who is building something that matters to them.*
