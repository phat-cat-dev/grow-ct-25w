## Part 0 Summary

### 0.1 Agent definition
<!-- brief: Two or three sentences for an agent: what this is and when to reach for it, and one thing it is not for. Write last, from the finished document. -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Understanding Learning, Assessment and Feedback is one of thirteen academic skills pathways: the mechanics of managing one's own learning at university, from how learning, assessment and feedback work, through working with the system (outcomes, alignment, marking criteria, the feedback cycle, self-regulated learning), to owning one's development within it (self- and peer-assessment, feedback dialogue, strategic use of assessment across a degree). Reach for it to place, sequence or assess a capability in learning how to learn, assessment literacy or feedback literacy, or to translate one into its workplace form. It is not about motivation, mindset and identity, which belong to Starting, Progressing and Succeeding, nor about reflection as a practice in its own right, which belongs to Reflective Practice.

### 0.2 Summary
<!-- brief: One paragraph: who proposed it, when, what it is, its main use and its main dispute. Write last. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
The academic skills pathways model (2026) sets Understanding Learning, Assessment and Feedback as one of thirteen pathways, drawn from four chapters of a study skills guide: on learning at university, on self-regulated learning, on assessment and on feedback. The model groups them because they reinforce each other: feedback cannot be understood without assessment, or either without how learning is designed. It is the "how" of managing one's own learning, beside a companion pathway's "why" and "who". It climbs in three tiers: how learning at university works (the discourse community, deep and surface learning, active learning, assessment and feedback broadly, the learning framework); working with the system (constructivism and behaviourism, intended learning outcomes and constructive alignment, marking criteria, the feedback cycle, the self-regulated learning phases); and owning one's development (self-assessment against criteria, peer assessment, feedback dialogue, assessment agility, strategic use of assessment across a degree). It sits beside established accounts of self-regulated learning, constructive alignment and feedback literacy, which the guide cites. Its open questions are an Advanced tier with no capability yet, and which theory to make key (Part E).

### A2 Core principles
<!-- brief: One item per claim the theory rests on, each cited to the authors. Claims only, not uses or benefits. -->
<!-- label: source; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Learning at university takes place within a discourse community, and the student's degree is a journey from its periphery towards its centre (D:EAU, pp. 28-30, after Swales, 1990 and Lave and Wenger, 1991).
- Knowledge is the outcome of interactions within the discourse community, not only data or information (D:EAU, p. 30).
- Tutors aim for deep-level learning, the development of wider, interrelated understanding, rather than surface-level learning of something specific for its own sake (D:EAU, pp. 32-33).
- Learning is designed on constructivist principles, with the tutor as a facilitator who empowers students to take control of their development; constructivism emerged from criticism of behaviourism's teacher-led training (D:EAU, pp. 35-37).
- Intended learning outcomes state what a student will get out of each activity, and activities and assessment are aligned to them (constructive alignment) to support students in driving their own learning (D:EAU, pp. 35, 38, after Biggs et al., 2022).
- Assessment is broader than tests and exams; from the tutor's side it is for learning (D:EAU, pp. 60-61, after Sambell et al., 2012).
- Formative assessment gives student and tutor insight into development and prepares for summative assessment; engaging with it sustains a self-regulated approach (D:EAU, pp. 42, 62-64, after Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick, 2006).
- Marking criteria set out what tutors look for across mark bands; applying them to one's own work is self-assessment (D:EAU, pp. 66-68).
- Feedback is comments on or insights into academic performance; it looks back at work submitted in order to look forward to work yet to come (D:EAU, pp. 76, 81, after Hattie and Timperley, 2007).
- Feedback is used through a cycle of four stages, review, interpret, articulate and apply, influenced by work on feedback literacy (D:EAU, pp. 81-82, after Carless and Boud, 2018).
- Self-regulated learning has three phases, forethought, behaviour and evaluation, in which the learner plans, monitors and reflects to improve (D:EAU, pp. 44, 50-52, after Zimmerman, 2002).

### A4 Concepts used
<!-- brief: The ideas the theory rests on that other theories share; each will become or match a TERMINOLOGY-DAT concept. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- discourse-community: a community of scholars focused on a discipline, which the student joins and moves through from novice to expert (D:EAU, pp. 28-30).
- academic-literacy: the ability to engage effectively with the business of the discourse community; an umbrella for all the literacies (D:EAU, p. 31, after Wingate, 2018).
- deep-learning: learning that develops wider, interrelated understanding (D:EAU, pp. 32-33).
- surface-learning: learning something specific for the sake of knowing it (D:EAU, pp. 32-33).
- constructivism: the theory that learners construct knowledge for themselves, the basis of student-centred learning (D:EAU, pp. 36-37).
- behaviourism: the theory that behaviour is developed through teacher-led training and repetition towards set outcomes (D:EAU, p. 37).
- intended-learning-outcome: a statement of what a student will get out of a learning activity (D:EAU, p. 35).
- constructive-alignment: designing activities and assessment to align with intended learning outcomes (D:EAU, p. 38).
- formative-assessment: a task that gives student and tutor insight into development, usually not counting towards the final mark (D:EAU, p. 42).
- summative-assessment: a task that counts towards the module result (D:EAU, p. 42).
- marking-criteria: the descriptors against which work is assessed in each mark band (D:EAU, p. 67).
- feedback: comments on or insights into academic performance (D:EAU, p. 76).
- self-regulated-learning: learning in which the learner plans, monitors and evaluates their own performance to improve it (D:EAU, pp. 44-45).
- asynchronous-learning: learning activities that are not live and can be done at a time that suits the student (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- curriculum: the components and structure of a course of study (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- discourse: the debate within a discipline, carried mainly in journal articles (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- knowledge: the interpretations, evidence and understanding that come out of a discipline's discourse (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- learning-framework: how the elements of a module's learning are organised to build towards its assessment (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- literacies: skills joined with an understanding of why each matters for learning at university (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- sense-making: understanding built by linking new knowledge to what one already knows (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- tutor-centred-learning: learning in which the tutor decides what is learnt and when, and students follow instructions (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- active-learning: learning based on the student's direct participation in the learning process (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- synchronous-learning: learning activities in which the student engages at the same time as the tutor and other students (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- authentic-assessment: assessment that re-creates a task students can expect in the workplace (D:EAU, p. 65).
- peer-assessment: students assessing each other's work, usually for a small share of the mark (D:EAU, pp. 66-67).
- feedback-dialogue: using feedback to open a conversation with the tutor about what it means and how to act on it (D:EAU, p. 80).

### A5a How practitioners apply it
<!-- brief: From the practitioner's side: the steps they take, the tools and artefacts they use, a worked example, and the decisions they make along the way. Separate what the authors prescribe (source) from how it is done in practice (reported, with sources) and what follows from it (editorial). -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
Practitioners here are tutors, academic skills staff and authors of learning material.

**What the authors prescribe** (source):
- Show how learning is designed from the tutor's side, including outcomes and alignment, so that students see why activities are set (D:EAU, pp. 34-39).
- Contrast two students' approaches to the same task, one self-regulating and one not, and ask what differs (D:EAU, pp. 46-50).
- Give simplified marking criteria and an exemplar, and ask learners to mark it (D:EAU, pp. 67-71).
- Teach the feedback cycle with a worked example from feedback to action plan (D:EAU, pp. 81-93).

**How it is done in practice** (reported):
- Tutors differ in what they comment on, though comments should align with the marking criteria (D:EAU, p. 77; evidence: reported).
- Most modules include a formative task (D:EAU, p. 42; evidence: reported).

**Worked example**:
At Introductory, the learner finds a module's outcomes, its formative task and its marking criteria and explains how each relates to learning. At Intermediate, they plan the summative task with goals and a schedule, monitor progress and adapt, and turn feedback on the formative task into an action plan. At Advanced, they mark their own draft against the criteria, ask the tutor about one descriptor, and carry their action plans forward across modules (editorial).

### A5b How beneficiaries meet it
<!-- brief: From the beneficiary's side (learner, client, project team, stakeholder): what it asks of them, what they see or do, and any version made for them to use directly. Search for this separately. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->

- Students tend to think of assessment as tests and exams (D:EAU, p. 60; evidence: claimed).
- A formative task that "doesn't count" is often given less effort, losing its value (D:EAU, p. 64; evidence: claimed).
- Students with a growth mindset use feedback to drive improvement; others make little constructive use of it (D:EAU, p. 81; evidence: claimed).

### A5c Contexts and adaptations
<!-- brief: Where it is used (levels, sectors, practices, settings, modes such as online), and how it has been adapted for them, each with its source. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- Any discipline; assessment forms and genres vary, and authentic and student-centred assessment are named (D:EAU, pp. 60, 65-66).
- Self-regulated learning: Zimmerman's cyclical model of forethought, performance and self-reflection phases, grounded in social cognitive theory (D:ZIMMERMAN; read by summary through Panadero and Alonso-Tapia, 2014).
- Feedback literacy: Carless and Boud (2018) define it as the understandings, capacities and dispositions needed to make sense of information and use it to enhance work or learning strategies, with four features: appreciating feedback, making judgements, managing affect and taking action (D:CARLESSBOUD; read by summary).
- Formative assessment and self-regulation: Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) argue that students already assess their own work and generate feedback, and set seven principles of good feedback practice (D:NICOL; read by summary; the principles not listed in the summary).
- Constructive alignment: Biggs (1996) (D:BIGGS, through META's run, read direct there).

### A5d Misapplications
<!-- brief: Common ways it is misused or misread, the evidence for each, and how to avoid it. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Treating a formative task as not worth doing because it does not count (D:EAU, pp. 62-64; evidence: claimed); treat it as practice and feedback for the summative task.
- Reading feedback for the mark alone (D:EAU, pp. 76, 81; evidence: claimed); work through the feedback cycle.
- Transcribing lectures rather than making notes actively (D:EAU, p. 51; evidence: claimed).
- Studying without planning or monitoring, then not reflecting (D:EAU, pp. 46-48; evidence: claimed).

### A8 Selection
<!-- brief: When to choose it over named alternatives, and what it suits badly. Our reading (editorial). -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Choose this pathway when the aim is to help a learner understand and work the system of learning, assessment and feedback and regulate their own study. For motivation, mindset and identity, choose `path:starting-progressing-succeeding`; for reflection as a practice, `path:reflective-practice`, which drives the feedback cycle; for exam and revision technique, `path:revising-exams`. It suits badly a learner whose need is a specific genre of assessment, which the genre pathways teach.
