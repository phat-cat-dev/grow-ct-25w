# Self-Development (academic skills pathway)

> **The core document.** Generic: it names no project that uses the pathway. Who made the model and where each claim comes from is in Part D. ## Part 0 Summary

### 0.1 Agent definition
<!-- brief: Two or three sentences for an agent: what this is and when to reach for it, and one thing it is not for. Write last, from the finished document. -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Self-Development is the fourteenth academic skills pathway: the practitioner identity of the academic practitioner, the student, from seeing oneself as someone who practises (graduate identity, a record of one's learning, naming one's values), through developing an identity within an academic community (participation from the edge to the centre, planning one's development against one's values, noticing how one's beliefs shape learning), to owning, articulating and examining that identity (evidence of identity and values, acting from organised values when they conflict, reflexivity). It climbs two ladders at once, the knowledge dimension towards the metacognitive and the affective domain from receiving to characterisation by a value system. Reach for it to place, sequence or assess a capability in a learner's identity or values; it is not about motivation and mindset (Starting, Progressing and Succeeding), the method of reflection (Reflective Practice) or the rules of integrity (Referencing).

### 0.2 Summary
<!-- brief: One paragraph: who proposed it, when, what it is, its main use and its main dispute. Write last. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
The academic skills pathways model's author added Self-Development on 4 October 2026 as a fourteenth pathway, about practitioner identity, for the academic practitioner first and the professional practitioner after (D-ing-0064). It has no tier map entry yet; its tiers are ing's draft (A3). It draws on the study skills guide's thread of graduate identity, which defines graduate identity, places each literacy's value in it, describes learning as a journey from the edge of a discourse community to its centre and frames integrity as a community's values (D:EAU), and on theory held as META runs: situated learning (Lave and Wenger), the affective domain (Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia), the self-system (Marzano and Kendall) and reflexivity (Finlay; D'Cruz and others). Its open questions are which theory to make key (self-authorship and identity in communities of practice are wanted, not held), how it is told apart from three neighbouring pathways, and the professional practitioner's version (Part E).

### A2 Core principles
<!-- brief: One item per claim the theory rests on, each cited to the authors. Claims only, not uses or benefits. -->
<!-- label: source; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Graduate identity is an identity defined by the competencies and capabilities associated with being a graduate (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- Each literacy has three sides: the skill (how), its significance (why) and its place within graduate identity (its value) (D:EAU, p. 3).
- Learning at university is a journey from the edge of a discourse community, as a novice, towards its centre, taking on the identity of an expert (D:EAU, p. 185, after Chapter 3).
- Academic integrity is a set of values that conditions how one engages with the work of one's community, as a student and later as a graduate in professional life (D:EAU, p. 187).
- Courage, as strength of character, is needed to act on one's values (D:EAU, p. 186).
- Reflection on oneself and one's approach to learning feeds personal development, turned into practical steps (D:EAU, pp. 55, 57).
- Identity capital is one's self-identity as a professional, the understanding of the value of one's experiences to one's aspirations (D:EAU, p. 249, after D:TOMLINSON).
- Learning is taking part in the practice of a community; newcomers change who they are as they move towards full membership (META run, D:LAVEWENGER).
- Values are internalised in an order: receiving, responding, valuing, organisation and characterisation by a value or value complex (META run, D:AFFECTIVE).
- Reflexivity is the continuing, self-aware evaluation of how the self shapes what one does and knows, practised in several ways from introspection to discursive deconstruction (META run, D:FINLAY).

### A4 Concepts used
<!-- brief: The ideas the theory rests on that other theories share; each will become or match a TERMINOLOGY-DAT concept. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- academic-practitioner: a student who practises the literacies of a discipline as a member of its community, not only one who studies (editorial, after D:EAU, pp. 3, 185).
- graduate-identity: an identity defined by the competencies and capabilities associated with being a graduate (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- practitioner-identity: who one is as a practitioner of a discipline or profession, formed by taking part in its practice (D:DIRECTION; after D:LAVEWENGER).
- discourse-community: the community of a discipline, whose discourse a student joins and moves through from edge to centre (D:EAU, pp. 28, 185; shared with `path:understanding-learning-assessment-feedback`).
- legitimate-peripheral-participation: learning by limited but genuine participation at the edge of a community, moving towards full membership (META run, D:LAVEWENGER).
- values: what a person holds important and acts from; in the affective domain, internalised from receiving to characterisation (META run, D:AFFECTIVE).
- characterisation-by-a-value: the highest level of the affective domain, at which a value system so governs behaviour that it marks the person (META run, D:AFFECTIVE).
- self-system: the system of thought that decides whether one engages with a task, by its importance, one's efficacy and one's emotional response (META run, D:MARZANO).
- identity-capital: one's self-identity as a professional, the understanding of the value of one's experiences to one's aspirations (D:EAU, p. 249, after D:TOMLINSON).
- reflexivity: continuing, self-aware evaluation of how one shapes what one knows and does (META run, D:FINLAY; shared with `path:reflective-practice`).
- self-authorship: the capacity to define one's own beliefs, identity and relationships rather than take them from others (after D:BAXTERMAGOLDA, by catalogue record; W-0063).

### A5a How practitioners apply it
<!-- brief: From the practitioner's side: the steps they take, the tools and artefacts they use, a worked example, and the decisions they make along the way. Separate what the authors prescribe (source) from how it is done in practice (reported, with sources) and what follows from it (editorial). -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
Practitioners here are personal tutors, academic skills staff, careers and employability staff, and authors of learning material.

**What the authors prescribe** (source):
- Relate each literacy to its place in graduate identity, not only to its skill and its use in assessment (D:EAU, p. 3).
- Reflect on what one learned about oneself, then turn it into practical steps for personal development (D:EAU, pp. 55, 57).
- Use graduate capital (human, social, cultural, identity, psychological) to relate study to one's growing sense of graduate identity (D:EAU, pp. 247-249).

**How it is done in practice** (reported):
- Personal development planning and personal tutoring in UK higher education (editorial; no source read).
- Portfolios and reflective statements of identity in professional programmes (editorial; no source read).

**Worked example**:
At Introductory, a student writes what they care about in their subject and keeps a short log of what they learn about themselves each week. At Intermediate, they notice they dismiss feedback that questions a belief, plan to test it, and act. At Advanced, they write a statement of their academic identity with evidence, hold to integrity when a group cuts corners, and examine how their background shaped their dissertation question (editorial).

### A5d Misapplications
<!-- brief: Common ways it is misused or misread, the evidence for each, and how to avoid it. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Treating identity as a fixed trait to be discovered rather than formed by practice (editorial, after D:LAVEWENGER); teach it as formed and changing.
- Telling students which values to hold (editorial); the pathway asks students to name, organise and examine their own, within the community's values of integrity.
- Assessing a student's identity or values as a person (editorial, after D:AFFECTIVE, which warns the affective domain is not a measure of a person); assess the practice of articulating, acting and examining, never the self.

### A7a Criticisms of the theory and its application
<!-- brief: Criticisms of the theory itself and of how practitioners apply it, with the evidence for each, marked measured, reported or claimed. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- none-stated: no published criticism of this pathway exists.
- (editorial) Values and identity are personal and culturally situated; a pathway that ranks them risks imposing one culture's ideal of the autonomous self. The cards rank the practice of examining values, not the values.
- (editorial) Reflexivity at the top may read as introspection only; Finlay's five variants and D'Cruz and others' three meanings include social critique and the production of knowledge, which the top card should keep in view.

### A8 Selection
<!-- brief: When to choose it over named alternatives, and what it suits badly. Our reading (editorial). -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Choose this pathway when the aim is the learner's identity and values as an academic practitioner. For motivation, mindset and the transition to and from university, choose `path:starting-progressing-succeeding`; for the method of reflecting, `path:reflective-practice`; for the rules and values of integrity, `path:referencing`; for identity formed on placement, `path:work-based-experiential-learning`. It suits badly a need for wellbeing support or counselling, which it is not.
