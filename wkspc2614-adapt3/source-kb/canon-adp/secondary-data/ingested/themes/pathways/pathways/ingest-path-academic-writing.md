## Part 0 Summary

### 0.1 Agent definition
<!-- brief: Two or three sentences for an agent: what this is and when to reach for it, and one thing it is not for. Write last, from the finished document. -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Academic Writing is one of thirteen academic skills pathways: writing essays and assignments for assessment, and understanding the conventions and style of academic writing, from getting a first draft down and getting the basics right, through clear expression, paragraph and essay structure and active use of sources, to writing within the conventions of one's own discipline. Reach for it to place, sequence or assess a writing capability, or to translate one into its workplace form. It signposts, and does not teach, reflective writing (Reflective Practice), critical research writing and the research genres (Researching and Research Methods) and report writing (placement open); citation mechanics belong to Referencing.

### 0.2 Summary
<!-- brief: One paragraph: who proposed it, when, what it is, its main use and its main dispute. Write last. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
The academic skills pathways model (2026) sets Academic Writing as one of thirteen pathways, drawn from a study skills guide's chapter on writing for assessment and the writing half of its chapter on critical thought. Its first move is demystification: academic writing is not one special genre, not different in kind from other writing, and not loftier; good academic writing is clear, precise and accessible. It climbs in three tiers: getting a draft down and the basics right (the writing cycle, the steer and keywords of a task, basic essay structure, common errors); writing with argument, structure and sources (clarity and the subject-verb-object model, topic and judgement sentences, the "voices" technique, the four pillars, the essay and assignment in a formal academic voice); and writing within one's discipline (reading for its conventions of style, evidence, vocabulary and discourse; the critical edge). It places discipline conventions last, where the guide puts them first. Its open questions are coverage, the line with Criticality and with Researching, and its theory links (Part E).

### A2 Core principles
<!-- brief: One item per claim the theory rests on, each cited to the authors. Claims only, not uses or benefits. -->
<!-- label: source; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Three common assumptions are false: that academic writing is a genre in itself, that it differs from other writing, and that it is inherently complex (D:EAU, p. 136).
- Good academic writing is often simple in sentence structure and everyday in vocabulary, with careful use of discipline terms (D:EAU, p. 136).
- Writing is shaped by the conventions of a discipline: style, vocabulary, structure, the sources used, how knowledge is handled and what the writing is for (D:EAU, p. 137).
- A learner comes to know a discipline's conventions by reading good research in it, attending to style, evidence, vocabulary and discourse (D:EAU, pp. 144-145).
- Students now need writing agility: writing well across the genres different assessments ask for (D:EAU, p. 145).
- An essay is a continuous response, usually an argument, to a question; its introduction, body and conclusion each have a job, and each paragraph is a microcosm of the essay (D:EAU, pp. 145-149).
- Reflective writing differs from other genres in using the first person and experience rather than the discourse (D:EAU, p. 161); the model teaches it under Reflective Practice, and this pathway refers to it briefly (D-ing-0022 (b)).
- Four features recur in marking criteria across genres: clarity and precision of expression; structure; effective engagement with the discourse; presentation, including references (D:EAU, p. 164).
- Writing is a process in stages: understand the task, plan, draft, edit, proofread; planning matters most (D:EAU, p. 165).
- It is better to have a draft to work with than nothing at all (D:EAU, p. 165).
- A plan is the bridge between research and writing, a live document carrying the line of reasoning (D:EAU, pp. 167-169).
- Editing makes substantive changes; proofreading removes superficial errors (D:EAU, pp. 170, 172).
- Sources are used actively: the writer's view first, sources supporting it, the writer's judgement last (D:EAU, pp. 179-180).

### A4 Concepts used
<!-- brief: The ideas the theory rests on that other theories share; each will become or match a TERMINOLOGY-DAT concept. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- writing-conventions: a discipline's expectations of style, vocabulary, structure, sources and handling of knowledge (D:EAU, p. 137).
- writing-within-the-disciplines: the view that writing support must be situated in the learner's discipline (D:EAU, p. 137, citing Halliday and Hasan 1985 and Somerville and Creme 2005).
- genre: a kind of writing tied to a kind of assessment, with its own expectations (D:EAU, pp. 136, 145).
- writing-agility: writing well across genres (D:EAU, p. 145).
- writing-cycle: the stages of producing assessed writing (D:EAU, p. 165).
- steer: the opening phrase of a question that sets the approach and the skill tested (D:EAU, p. 165).
- line-of-reasoning: the argument in its basic form, set out early in a plan (D:EAU, p. 167).
- topic-sentence: a paragraph's first sentence, stating its topic and its link to the argument (D:EAU, pp. 148, 178).
- active-voice: a sentence whose subject does the action (D:EAU, p. 175).
- voices: a paragraph technique alternating the writer's voice with those of the sources (D:EAU, pp. 179-180).
- discourse: the academic debate in a discipline's sources (D:EAU, p. 145).
- critical-edge: writing that makes critical engagement visible (D:EAU, p. 181).
- academic-voice: the formal register of argued academic writing, usually in the third person, building an argument through the discourse (D:EAU, pp. 137, 161).
- conventions: the characteristic features of writing in a discipline (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- flow-in-writing: in writing, the sense of progression and connection between sentences in a paragraph (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- language-of-criticality: words and phrases that signal critical use of sources, usually in writing (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- synthesis: drawing evidence and often conflicting interpretations together to express one's own view (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- essay: a continuous written response, usually an argument, to an assessment question, with an introduction, body and conclusion (D:EAU, p. 145).
- plan: the outline that carries the line of reasoning between research and writing (D:EAU, pp. 165, 167-169).
- first-draft: the first complete version of a piece of writing, made from the plan (D:EAU, p. 165).
- editing: making substantive changes to a draft: expression, structure and evidence (D:EAU, pp. 170-172).
- proofreading: removing superficial errors from a draft before submission (D:EAU, pp. 165, 172).
- keyword: a word in an assessment question that names the topic the answer must address, read with the steer (D:EAU, pp. 165-167).
- macro-structure: the arrangement of the main points across a whole piece of writing (D:EAU, p. 177).
- micro-structure: the structure of individual paragraphs, including the introduction and conclusion (D:EAU, p. 177).
- passive-voice: a sentence whose subject receives the action, which has less impact than the active (D:EAU, p. 176).
- svo-model: building a sentence as subject, verb, object, the simplest clear sentence structure (D:EAU, p. 175).
- reflective-writing: writing about experience in the first person, less formally than argued academic writing, but still critical (D:EAU, pp. 137, 161).

### A5a How practitioners apply it
<!-- brief: From the practitioner's side: the steps they take, the tools and artefacts they use, a worked example, and the decisions they make along the way. Separate what the authors prescribe (source) from how it is done in practice (reported, with sources) and what follows from it (editorial). -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
Practitioners here are tutors, academic skills staff, librarians and authors of learning material.

**What the authors prescribe** (source):
- Read discipline exemplars side by side and ask learners to characterise style, evidence, vocabulary and discourse (D:EAU, pp. 138-145).
- Model the parts of an essay with an annotated introduction, body and conclusion (D:EAU, pp. 146-149).
- Show editing through an unedited and an edited version of the same paragraph (D:EAU, pp. 170-172).
- Ask learners to rewrite a weak paragraph of their own with the voices technique (D:EAU, p. 180).

**Cross-reference** (editorial, D-ing-0026): where this pathway treats formulating an argument (the line of reasoning in the plan, the topic and judgement sentences), it discusses and refers to Criticality's argument plan (formulating one's own argument) and argument analysis (breaking down other people's) (D:CRIT, C4).

**How it is done in practice** (reported):
- Writing support is increasingly situated within disciplines (D:EAU, p. 137; evidence: reported).
- Some essays are written by essay mills or with generative AI, which has put the essay in question as an assessment (D:EAU, pp. 145-146; evidence: reported).

**Worked example**:
At Introductory, the learner identifies a question's steer and keywords, plans, drafts quickly and proofreads for comma splices. At Intermediate, they edit for clarity, give each paragraph a topic sentence and a closing judgement, and use sources through the voices technique. At Advanced, they read three articles in their discipline for its conventions and revise their own writing to match (editorial).

### A5c Contexts and adaptations
<!-- brief: Where it is used (levels, sectors, practices, settings, modes such as online), and how it has been adapted for them, each with its source. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- Any discipline; the guide's exemplars stand for the sciences (chemistry), social sciences (education) and humanities (history) (D:EAU, p. 138).
- Writing outside assessment (tutorial preparation, emails to tutors, research notes) is practice for it (D:EAU, p. 137).
- Students writing in English as an additional language more often miss or confuse articles (D:EAU, p. 172).
- Student writing research: Lea and Street (1998) set three models of supporting student writing, study skills, academic socialisation and academic literacies, the last treating writing as situated and contested practice (D:LEASTREET; read by summary).
- Writing process research: Flower and Hayes (1981) model writing as planning, translating and reviewing, moved between by a monitor (D:FLOWERHAYES; read by summary).
- Genre research: Swales (1990) analyses academic genres by their moves within discourse communities, as in the research article introduction (D:SWALES; read by summary).

### A5d Misapplications
<!-- brief: Common ways it is misused or misread, the evidence for each, and how to avoid it. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Treating academic writing as a lofty special genre and writing to sound complex (D:EAU, p. 136; evidence: claimed); avoid by aiming at clarity.
- Trying to write the finished piece in one go and deleting each attempt (D:EAU, pp. 164-165; evidence: claimed); get a draft down first.
- Writing without a plan (D:EAU, p. 165; evidence: claimed).
- Copying each paragraph's first sentence into the conclusion (D:EAU, p. 149; evidence: claimed); synthesise instead.
- Reporting sources rather than using them (D:EAU, pp. 179, 181; evidence: claimed).
- Ending a paragraph without a judgement (D:EAU, p. 178; evidence: claimed).

### A8 Selection
<!-- brief: When to choose it over named alternatives, and what it suits badly. Our reading (editorial). -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Choose this pathway when the aim is to write essays and assignments well and in the academic style. For the critical engagement behind the writing, choose `path:criticality`, which teaches the critical edge; for critical research writing (proposals, literature reviews, research reports), `path:researching-research-methods`; for citing, `path:referencing`; for reflective writing and the reflective cycle behind it, `path:reflective-practice`. This pathway signposts each of them. Report writing is mentioned and signposted, its home not yet decided. It suits badly a learner whose need is spoken or visual communication, which belongs to `path:presenting`.
