## Part 0 Summary

### 0.1 Agent definition
<!-- brief: Two or three sentences for an agent: what this is and when to reach for it, and one thing it is not for. Write last, from the finished document. -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Presenting is one of thirteen academic skills pathways: creating and delivering presentations as assessment and as a transferable skill, from how a presentation is put together (five steps, structure), through delivering it well across formats (visual aids and accessibility, nerves, individual, poster, group, professional, interview-based and technology-based presentations), to presenting with authority and working in groups (academic voice, defending an argument under questioning, professional formats, leadership and conflict resolution). Reach for it to place, sequence or assess a capability in oral and visual communication or group work, or to translate one into its workplace form. It is not about writing (Academic Writing), nor about reflection as a practice, which the group work's reflective element draws on (Reflective Practice).

### 0.2 Summary
<!-- brief: One paragraph: who proposed it, when, what it is, its main use and its main dispute. Write last. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
The academic skills pathways model (2026) sets Presenting as one of thirteen pathways, drawn from one chapter of a study skills guide on creating presentations and working in groups. It climbs in three tiers: what a presentation is and how one is put together (reading the brief, the five steps, a hook, one point per slide, a conclusion that returns to the objectives); delivering it well across formats (the six types of presentation, "less is more" and accessible visual aids, managing nerves and avoiding the classic errors); and presenting with authority and collaborating (academic voice, asserting and defending an argument under questioning, the pitch, the conference poster and the viva-style interview in depth, and group work: personality types, leadership and conflict resolution, and the reflective element tutors assess). The guide frames both presentations and group work as transferable skills and parts of graduate identity, often tested at interview. The model flags that group work could become a pathway of its own and that the pathway is less writing-centric than most, to be pressure-tested before it is built. Its open questions are an Introductory tier with no capability, the place of group work and which theory to make key (Part E).

### A2 Core principles
<!-- brief: One item per claim the theory rests on, each cited to the authors. Claims only, not uses or benefits. -->
<!-- label: source; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Presentations test many of the literacies of written work, argument, critique of the discourse and clear communication, but orally, in front of an audience, with visual aids and constraints such as a time limit and questions (D:EAU, p. 201).
- A presentation develops the student's academic voice: asserting and defending an argument under questioning gives ownership of one's understanding and disciplinary knowledge (D:EAU, p. 201).
- Presenting and working in groups are transferable skills valued by employers and often tested at interview; a presentation in selection is a statement of graduate identity (D:EAU, pp. 200, 202).
- Tutors use different types of presentation, each with its own expectations: individual, poster, group, professional or practice-based, interview-based and technology-based (D:EAU, pp. 203-204).
- A presentation is created in five steps: read the instructions, research the topic, plan, design visual aids, then put it together and practise (D:EAU, pp. 206-208).
- Visual aids complement the spoken content and should not distract; "less is more", and design should consider accessibility for visually impaired and dyslexic audiences (D:EAU, pp. 208-209).
- A presentation has an introduction with a hook and signposted objectives, a main body arranged logically with one point per slide, and a conclusion that returns to the objectives (D:EAU, pp. 209-210).
- Nerves are normal; project confidence, engage the audience, know the room and its technology, keep to time and prepare for questions (D:EAU, pp. 206, 210-212).
- In group work tutors value the process over the product, and assessment often includes a reflective element on one's contribution (D:EAU, pp. 212-213).
- Awareness of personality types, after the five-factor model, helps a group member resolve conflict and work with others (D:EAU, pp. 216-217, after McCrae and Costa, 1999). [The pathway does not teach it: group work is taught through process (D-ing-0054).]

### A4 Concepts used
<!-- brief: The ideas the theory rests on that other theories share; each will become or match a TERMINOLOGY-DAT concept. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- presentation: an assessed or professional activity in which a student communicates an argument orally to an audience, usually with visual aids and within constraints (D:EAU, p. 201).
- visual-aid: slides or other visuals that complement the spoken content (D:EAU, pp. 207-208).
- hook: an opening device, such as an anecdote or a challenge to an accepted view, that captures the audience's attention (D:EAU, p. 209).
- academic-voice: the student's own voice in the discourse, developed by asserting and defending an argument (D:EAU, p. 201).
- transferable-skills: skills or competencies that carry across professional contexts (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii; p. 200).
- group-work: assessed or unassessed work completed with other students, valued for its process (D:EAU, pp. 212-213).
- personality-type: in the guide's use, a pattern of behaviour in a group read from the five factors (D:EAU, pp. 216-217).
- leadership: organising, deciding, mediating and motivating within a team (D:EAU, p. 215).
- accountability: taking responsibility for the team's decisions and work (D:EAU, p. 215).
- resilience: staying focused on the task through conflict and obstacles (D:EAU, p. 214).
- academic-authority: the confidence and standing to assert and defend one's argument in a discipline (D:EAU, pp. 200-201, chapter keywords).
- collaboration: working with others towards a shared product, valued by employers (D:EAU, pp. 200, 212, chapter keywords).

### A5a How practitioners apply it
<!-- brief: From the practitioner's side: the steps they take, the tools and artefacts they use, a worked example, and the decisions they make along the way. Separate what the authors prescribe (source) from how it is done in practice (reported, with sources) and what follows from it (editorial). -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
Practitioners here are tutors, academic skills staff and authors of learning material.

**What the authors prescribe** (source):
- Set a worked brief and contrast a poorly prepared approach with the five steps (Natalie's social media strategy) (D:EAU, pp. 205-208).
- Show a failed delivery and ask what went wrong (Nasser) (D:EAU, pp. 210-212).
- Give design recommendations for visual aids, with accessibility (D:EAU, pp. 208-209).
- Use a group conflict and its resolution to discuss personality types and leadership (Ross) (D:EAU, pp. 215-217).
- Ask learners to reflect on a group activity with a three-stage cycle and place themselves among the five factors (D:EAU, p. 217).

**How it is done in practice** (reported):
- Tutors assess content, communication, delivery, visual aids and handling of questions (D:EAU, p. 203; evidence: reported).
- Employers include a presentation task in selection, judging communication more than content (D:EAU, p. 202; evidence: reported).
- Group assessment often includes a reflective element on the process (D:EAU, p. 213; evidence: reported).

**Worked example**:
At Introductory, the learner reads a brief, plans eight slides with a hook, three points and a conclusion, and practises to time. At Intermediate, they redesign cluttered slides for legibility and accessibility, rehearse a strategy for nerves and recast the talk as a poster. At Advanced, they deliver a pitch to a panel, defend it under questioning and, in a group, mediate a conflict and reflect on their own contribution (editorial).

### A5b How beneficiaries meet it
<!-- brief: From the beneficiary's side (learner, client, project team, stakeholder): what it asks of them, what they see or do, and any version made for them to use directly. Search for this separately. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Many students find public speaking nerve-wracking, especially before an unfamiliar or senior audience (D:EAU, p. 206; evidence: claimed).
- Group presentations are often a cause of anxiety (D:EAU, p. 203; evidence: claimed).
- Conscientious students are often the most frustrated by group work, because their mark depends on others (D:EAU, pp. 212-213; evidence: claimed).
- Students focused on marks find it hard to see that tutors value the group's process over its product (D:EAU, p. 213; evidence: claimed).

### A5c Contexts and adaptations
<!-- brief: Where it is used (levels, sectors, practices, settings, modes such as online), and how it has been adapted for them, each with its source. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- Any discipline; vocational disciplines use professional or practice-based presentations that mirror the workplace, assessed against professional standards and ethics (D:EAU, p. 204).
- Research presentations: dissertation progress updates and conference posters (D:EAU, pp. 203-204).
- Online: virtual presentations and recorded video, assessed on engaging remote audiences and digital skills (D:EAU, p. 204).
- Visual aids: Mayer's principles of multimedia learning (coherence, signalling, redundancy and others) give an evidence base for "less is more" (D:MAYER; read by summary).
- Presentation anxiety: communication apprehension, "the anxiety resulting from fear of public speaking", after McCroskey (1977) (D:MCCROSKEY, by summary); Bodie (2010) reviews how it is defined, explained and treated (D:BODIE, by citation).
- Slide design: the assertion-evidence approach, a sentence headline with visual evidence, gave better comprehension and fewer misconceptions than bulleted slides with the same talk (D:GARNERALLEY, by summary).
- Group work in higher education: cooperative learning's five elements, including individual accountability (D:JOHNSON, by summary); social loafing, effort lost in groups when contributions are not tracked (D:LATANE, by summary); team psychological safety (D:EDMONDSON, by summary); Tuckman's stages (D:TUCKMAN, by summary).

### A5d Misapplications
<!-- brief: Common ways it is misused or misread, the evidence for each, and how to avoid it. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Little research or planning, so the presentation lacks depth and coherence (D:EAU, p. 206; evidence: claimed); follow the five steps.
- Cluttered slides read aloud, with the back to the audience (D:EAU, pp. 208, 211; evidence: claimed); keep slides to keywords and look at the audience.
- Reading from fully scripted cards (D:EAU, pp. 210-211; evidence: claimed); use bullet-point prompts.
- Overrunning and dropping content (D:EAU, p. 211; evidence: claimed); practise to time.
- Using unfamiliar software to impress (D:EAU, p. 208; evidence: claimed).

### A7a Criticisms of the theory and its application
<!-- brief: Criticisms of the theory itself and of how practitioners apply it, with the evidence for each, marked measured, reported or claimed. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- none-stated: no published criticism of this pathway exists.
- (editorial) The guide's reading of the five-factor model departs from it. The model treats the five as continuous traits, not types, and defines neuroticism as a tendency to unpleasant emotions such as anxiety and vulnerability; the guide calls neuroticism "realists" and maps each factor to a team role (D:EAU, pp. 216-217; against D:BIGFIVE, read by summary). Part E, item 6.
- (editorial) The guide cites no theory of presenting itself (rhetoric, communication apprehension, multimedia design); the pathway rests on the authors' practice (Part E, item 4).
- (editorial) Group work sits in the Advanced tier of a presenting pathway; the model itself flags that it may become its own pathway (Part E, item 5).

### A8 Selection
<!-- brief: When to choose it over named alternatives, and what it suits badly. Our reading (editorial). -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Choose this pathway when the aim is oral and visual communication of academic work, or working with others on an assessed task. For writing, choose `path:academic-writing`; for reflecting on a group task, `path:reflective-practice`; for professional practice settings, `path:work-based-experiential-learning`; for research posters' content, `path:researching-research-methods`.
