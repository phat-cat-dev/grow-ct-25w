## Part 0 Summary

### 0.1 Agent definition
<!-- brief: Two or three sentences for an agent: what this is and when to reach for it, and one thing it is not for. Write last, from the finished document. -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Starting, Progressing and Succeeding is one of thirteen academic skills pathways and the model's entry point: the dispositional layer of studying, from finding one's feet (the conventions of academic practice, mindset, motivation, goals, self-efficacy), through keeping going (sustaining motivation, handling setbacks and anxiety, seeking support early), to becoming a graduate (independence, graduate identity and capital, lifelong learning). Reach for it to place, sequence or assess a capability in the "why" and "who" of studying, or to translate one into its workplace form. It is not about the mechanics of managing one's learning (outcomes, criteria, feedback cycles, self-regulated learning phases), which belong to Understanding Learning, Assessment and Feedback.

### 0.2 Summary
<!-- brief: One paragraph: who proposed it, when, what it is, its main use and its main dispute. Write last. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
The academic skills pathways model (2026) sets Starting, Progressing and Succeeding as the first of thirteen pathways and the programme's entry point, drawn from the opening and closing chapters of a study skills guide: on mindset, motivation and goals, and on becoming a graduate. It is the dispositional layer, who the student is becoming and why they are motivated, beside a companion pathway's mechanics of learning. It climbs in three tiers: starting (familiarising oneself with the conventions of academic practice, conforming to them and then expressing oneself within them, as amended by the model's author; what excelling means, the move from school or college to university, growth and fixed mindsets, mastery and performance motivation, goals and sub-goals, self-efficacy); progressing (sustaining motivation across a course, handling setbacks, disappointment and anxiety, realistic expectations without perfectionism, seeking support early); and succeeding (independence and self-direction, graduate identity, the move into professional life, lifelong learning). It rests on accounts of mindset, self-efficacy, achievement goals and graduate capital, which the guide cites. Its theory is Dweck, Bandura, Pintrich and Tomlinson; its main dispute is the weak evidence for growth mindset interventions (Part E).

- Three tiers, Introductory (starting), Intermediate (progressing) and Advanced (succeeding), each with a focus, content, a progression logic and links to other pathways (D:PTM, Pathway 1).
- Progression runs from finding one's feet, to sustaining and adapting, to becoming a graduate (D:PTM, Pathway 1).
- The pathway is the programme's entry point, surfaced first, introducing the others and carrying the orientation tone; every other pathway leans on it for the motivational and dispositional layer (D:PTM, Pathway 1).
- It is the dispositional layer, distinct from the mechanics of learning, whose self-regulated learning is its mechanical counterpart (D:PTM, Pathway 1).
- Tiers are anchored to experience of the practice, not to year of study (D:TSPEC, section 2).
- Across the tiers runs the evolution of the learner's self-identity as an academic practitioner: from learning and conforming to the conventions of academic practice, through expressing oneself within them and investing in one's development, to directing one's own practice independently; the pathway therefore carries metacognition (D-ing-0046).

### A2 Core principles
<!-- brief: One item per claim the theory rests on, each cited to the authors. Claims only, not uses or benefits. -->
<!-- label: source; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Success at university is subjective and has two sides: academic performance (marks, and the steps towards mastery of the discipline) and personal development (the attributes of graduate identity) (D:EAU, pp. 10-15).
- Excelling is exceeding expectations: achieving one's goals and more, and making tangible growth in competencies and behaviours (D:EAU, pp. 15-16).
- Finding success and excelling depend on three related factors: mindset, motivation and goals (D:EAU, p. 16).
- A growth mindset links effort to success and treats assessment and feedback as chances to develop; a fixed mindset treats ability as given (D:EAU, pp. 16-19, after Yeager and Dweck, 2012).
- Self-efficacy, belief in one's capacity to do what a task needs, underpins mindset, motivation and goals, and can be built through overcoming challenges, seeing peers succeed, positive feedback and managing stress and anxiety (D:EAU, pp. 19-21, after Bandura, 1977, 1994).
- Motivation is broadly towards performance (marks, comparison with others) or learning (mastery and personal development); it extends from mindset, rests on self-efficacy and on the value one places on a task, and fluctuates (D:EAU, pp. 21-23, after Dweck, 2012).
- Goals emerge from motivation; mastery goals serve learning and development better than targeted (performance) goals, and achievable goals sustain motivation (D:EAU, pp. 23-25, after Pintrich, 2005 and Zimmerman, Bandura and Martinez-Pons, 1992).
- Moving from the structured learning of school or college to directing one's own learning is one of the most significant challenges of the transition into higher education (D:EAU, p. 44).
- Becoming a graduate means building graduate capital in five related forms, human, social, cultural, identity and psychological, and being able to articulate it (D:EAU, pp. 247-251, after Tomlinson, 2017).

### A4 Concepts used
<!-- brief: The ideas the theory rests on that other theories share; each will become or match a TERMINOLOGY-DAT concept. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- success: achievement judged by academic performance and by personal development towards graduate identity (D:EAU, pp. 10-15).
- excelling: exceeding one's own expectations, achieving goals and more (D:EAU, p. 15).
- mindset: one's attitude to studying and beliefs about one's abilities and chances of success, shaped by experience (D:EAU, pp. 16-17).
- growth-mindset: the belief that ability develops through effort and practice (D:EAU, pp. 17-18, after Yeager and Dweck, 2012).
- fixed-mindset: the belief that ability is given and fixed (D:EAU, pp. 17-18, after Yeager and Dweck, 2012).
- self-efficacy: belief in one's own capacity to work effectively to complete a task or reach a goal (D:EAU, p. 19, after Bandura, 1977).
- motivation: what pushes and sustains engagement with one's course (D:EAU, p. 16).
- mastery-goal: a far-reaching goal aimed at learning and development (D:EAU, p. 24, after Pintrich, 2005).
- performance-goal: a specific goal aimed at a result such as a mark; the guide calls it a targeted goal (D:EAU, p. 24).
- graduate-attributes: competencies and behaviours that apply across disciplines, set out in each university's framework (D:EAU, pp. 13-14).
- graduate-identity: the attributes and self-understanding of a graduate, developed through the degree (D:EAU, pp. 9, 246).
- graduate-capital: the resources, human, social, cultural, identity and psychological, that underpin graduate employability (D:EAU, pp. 247-249, after Tomlinson, 2017).
- employability: achievements, skills, understanding and attributes that make graduates more likely to gain the work they seek (D:EAU, p. 13, after Yorke, 2006).
- lifelong-learning: learning as an ongoing process that continues in the workplace (D:EAU, p. 44).
- academic-literacy: all the literacies a student needs to take part in a discourse community's work (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- add-on-provision: optional extra-curricular sessions, such as library search workshops, that sit beside the formal learning framework (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- conventions: the characteristic features of writing in a discipline (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- literacies: skills joined with an understanding of why each matters for learning at university (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- mastery: expert-level knowledge and understanding (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- transferable-skills: skills or competencies that carry across professional contexts (D:EAU, glossary, pp. x-xiii).
- mastery-experience: succeeding at a task oneself, the strongest of Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy (after Bandura, 1977; read by summary; W-0054).

### A5a How practitioners apply it
<!-- brief: From the practitioner's side: the steps they take, the tools and artefacts they use, a worked example, and the decisions they make along the way. Separate what the authors prescribe (source) from how it is done in practice (reported, with sources) and what follows from it (editorial). -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
Practitioners here are tutors, personal tutors, academic skills and careers staff and authors of learning material.

**What the authors prescribe** (source):
- Contrast two students with the same task, one with a fixed mindset and performance motivation, one with a growth mindset and learning motivation, and ask whose approach is closer to the learner's (D:EAU, pp. 17-19, 21-23).
- Ask learners to name the week's tasks, a goal and what success would look like, as a diary entry (D:EAU, pp. 12-13).
- Ask learners to assess their self-efficacy by reviewing a recent challenge (D:EAU, p. 21).
- Ask learners to re-phrase performance goals as mastery goals (D:EAU, pp. 24-25).
- Ask learners to link a recent learning experience to their university's graduate attributes (D:EAU, p. 15).
- Ask learners to assess their graduate capital in each of five forms, plan how to develop each and role-play articulating it to an employer (D:EAU, pp. 249-251).

**How it is done in practice** (reported):
- Each university has its own graduate attributes framework, which informs module learning objectives (D:EAU, p. 13; evidence: reported).
- Growth mindset interventions are widely used in schools; their measured effects on achievement are small on average and larger for disadvantaged and academically at-risk students (D:SISK; read by summary).

**Worked example**:
At Introductory, the learner compares Tom's and Saeideh's approaches to a first assessment, places their own mindset and motivation, and rewrites three goals as mastery goals broken into weekly steps. At Intermediate, after a disappointing mark, they review what happened, name the stress it caused, seek a tutor's help that week and reset achievable goals. At Advanced, they audit their graduate capital in five forms with evidence from a placement and a dissertation and rehearse explaining it in an interview (editorial).

### A5b How beneficiaries meet it
<!-- brief: From the beneficiary's side (learner, client, project team, stakeholder): what it asks of them, what they see or do, and any version made for them to use directly. Search for this separately. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Students coming straight from school or college may find directing their own learning alien (D:EAU, p. 44; evidence: claimed).
- Students with low self-efficacy tend to attribute failure to low ability, which reinforces it; those with high self-efficacy attribute it to effort or strategy (D:EAU, p. 20, after Bandura; evidence: claimed).
- Some students aim only to "do enough" to get by (D:EAU, p. 22; evidence: claimed).
- Students with a fixed mindset may dismiss feedback and blame the tutor after a low mark (D:EAU, pp. 17-18; evidence: claimed).

### A5c Contexts and adaptations
<!-- brief: Where it is used (levels, sectors, practices, settings, modes such as online), and how it has been adapted for them, each with its source. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- Any discipline, from the first weeks of a degree to postgraduate study; the guide notes that challenges recur in a final-year dissertation and on a postgraduate course (D:EAU, p. 11).
- Self-efficacy: Bandura (1977) names four sources, mastery experiences (the strongest), vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological and emotional states, and separates efficacy expectations from outcome expectations (D:BANDURA1977; read by summary).
- Student persistence: Tinto's later work moves from academic and social integration to the student's motivation to persist, shaped by goals, self-efficacy, sense of belonging and the perceived value of the curriculum (D:TINTO, through META's run, by summary there).
- Transition as a psychological process of ending, neutral zone and new beginning, from managing change in organisations (D:BRIDGES, through META's run).
- Graduate employability as five forms of capital acquired through formal and informal experience (D:TOMLINSON; read by summary).
- First-year transition: Kift's transition pedagogy sets six first-year curriculum principles (transition, diversity, design, engagement, assessment, evaluation and monitoring) (D:KIFT; read by summary).

### A5d Misapplications
<!-- brief: Common ways it is misused or misread, the evidence for each, and how to avoid it. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Setting only targeted goals tied to a mark (D:EAU, p. 24; evidence: claimed); re-phrase them as mastery goals.
- Ignoring extra-curricular and employability sessions because no assessment needs them (D:EAU, pp. 21-22; evidence: claimed); weigh their value for development.
- Putting off a task one feels less confident about, so that a low mark can be blamed on lack of effort (D:EAU, p. 22; evidence: claimed).
- (editorial) Teaching growth mindset as a slogan ("just try harder") without the strategies, feedback and support that make effort pay; the evidence for mindset messages alone is weak (D:SISK).

Beneficiaries: students; later, employers.

### A7a Criticisms of the theory and its application
<!-- brief: Criticisms of the theory itself and of how practitioners apply it, with the evidence for each, marked measured, reported or claimed. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- none-stated: no published criticism of this pathway exists.
- Growth mindset: two meta-analyses found a very weak relation between mindset and achievement (r = .10) and a small average intervention effect (d = 0.08) (D:SISK; evidence: measured, read by summary).
- Student persistence: integration models are said to put too much on the individual and to overlook finance, culture and non-traditional students (D:TINTO, through META's run; evidence: claimed).
- (editorial) The guide's account of disposition centres the individual student's mindset and motivation; structural barriers to success (money, caring, work, belonging) are not discussed.
- (editorial) The Intermediate tier's setbacks, anxiety, perfectionism and support-seeking have little treatment in the guide's two chapters and no card (Part E, items 3 and 7).

### A7b Effects on beneficiaries

Beneficiaries: students.
- unknown: no evidence yet of how learners fare in the pathway.
- (editorial) Framing success as mindset and effort risks implying that students who struggle lack effort; the pathway needs to pair it with support-seeking.

### A8 Selection
<!-- brief: When to choose it over named alternatives, and what it suits badly. Our reading (editorial). -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Choose this pathway when the aim is a learner's disposition: what success means to them, their mindset, motivation, goals and confidence, keeping going, and seeing themselves as a graduate. For planning, monitoring and evaluating study, outcomes, criteria and feedback, choose `path:understanding-learning-assessment-feedback`; for reflection as a practice, `path:reflective-practice`; for learning on placement and professional identity in practice, `path:work-based-experiential-learning`; for exam anxiety and revision, `path:revising-exams`. It suits badly a learner who needs welfare or mental health support, which it can point to but not give.
