## Part 0 Summary

### 0.1 Agent definition
<!-- brief: Two or three sentences for an agent: what this is and when to reach for it, and one thing it is not for. Write last, from the finished document. -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Referencing, Citation and Academic Integrity is one of thirteen academic skills pathways: acknowledging where knowledge comes from, from the mechanics of in-text references and a references list in one consistent style, through quotation, paraphrase and citation as evidence of scope and engagement, to the values of academic integrity as part of graduate and professional identity. Reach for it to place, sequence or assess a referencing or integrity capability, or to translate one into its workplace form; it also carries the managing of sources as they are found. It is not about research ethics in the field, which belongs to Researching, nor about the integrity questions raised by AI tools, which belong to the AI and technology pathway.

### 0.2 Summary
<!-- brief: One paragraph: who proposed it, when, what it is, its main use and its main dispute. Write last. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
The academic skills pathways model (2026) sets Referencing, Citation and Academic Integrity as one of thirteen pathways, drawn from a study skills guide's chapter on academic integrity and referencing. The model reverses the guide's order: the guide opens with integrity and its values and comes to referencing after, while the pathway starts with the mechanics, moves to citation and plagiarism and ends with the values. It climbs in three tiers: getting referencing right (why we reference, in-text references and the references list, author-date mechanics for the common source types, accuracy and consistency, copy-paste errors, basic plagiarism awareness); citation and plagiarism (citation as evidence of scope, critical engagement and disciplinary knowledge, quotation against paraphrase, trickier sources); and academic integrity (the six fundamental values, intentional and unintentional plagiarism, integrity as graduate and professional identity). It sits beside the sector's statements of integrity values and the information literacy models, which place citing within managing information. Its open questions are coverage at Introductory and Intermediate, the managing of sources it now carries, and which theory to link (Part E).

### A2 Core principles
<!-- brief: One item per claim the theory rests on, each cited to the authors. Claims only, not uses or benefits. -->
<!-- label: source; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Academic integrity is a literacy in its own right, made more pressing by essay mills and generative AI (D:EAU, p. 184).
- Learning is a journey from the edge of a discourse community, as a novice, towards its centre; integrity is part of the identity taken on along the way (D:EAU, p. 185).
- Academic integrity rests on six fundamental values: honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and courage (D:EAU, pp. 185-187, after D:ICAI).
- Integrity is more than avoiding plagiarism: the values inform conduct in professional contexts and graduate identity (D:EAU, p. 187).
- Referencing acknowledges where knowledge comes from, and is also a tool for meeting marking criteria (D:EAU, p. 188).
- Styles differ by discipline and by institution; the one required is checked in the module handbook or with the tutor, and used consistently and accurately (D:EAU, pp. 188-189).
- An in-text reference marks each use of a source; the references list gives every source used, in alphabetical order by author surname (D:EAU, pp. 189-191).
- Plagiarism is presenting another's words or ideas as one's own without acknowledgement; it is not always deliberate, but unintentional plagiarism comes from cutting corners, such as pasting text to reference later (D:EAU, p. 192).
- References show the reader the scope of the research and the engagement with the discourse; tutors often read the references list first (D:EAU, pp. 191-193).
- References signpost the critical edge and display disciplinary knowledge (D:EAU, pp. 192-193).
- Quotations should be kept to a minimum, because depending on them undermines the demonstration of understanding (D:EAU, pp. 193-194).
- Paraphrase restates a source's point in one's own words: identify the key point, disregard the rest, write it in your own words, and reference it (D:EAU, pp. 194-195).
- A references list contains only the sources cited; a bibliography also lists sources consulted (D:EAU, p. 196).
- Referencing's broader value is as the learner's means of demonstrating engagement with the relevant discourse (D:EAU, p. 6).
- Knowledge is the outcome of the interactions within a discipline's discourse community; a student joins that community and journeys from its periphery to its centre (D:EAU, pp. 29-30, after Lave and Wenger, 1991).

### A4 Concepts used
<!-- brief: The ideas the theory rests on that other theories share; each will become or match a TERMINOLOGY-DAT concept. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- academic-integrity: acting on the fundamental values in all academic work (D:EAU, pp. 184-187).
- discourse-community: the community of a discipline, which a learner joins as a novice and moves towards the centre of (D:EAU, p. 185).
- plagiarism: presenting another's words or ideas as one's own without acknowledgement, deliberately or not (D:EAU, p. 192).
- referencing: acknowledging the sources of knowledge used, in the text and in a references list (D:EAU, p. 188).
- referencing-style: a set convention for the form of references, such as author-date, MHRA, Chicago, APA, Vancouver, IEEE or OSCOLA (D:EAU, pp. 188-189).
- quotation: another's exact words, marked as such and referenced (D:EAU, pp. 193-194).
- paraphrase: another's point restated in one's own words and referenced (D:EAU, pp. 194-195).
- scope: the range of research done, shown to the reader by the references (D:EAU, p. 191).
- graduate-identity: the professional and personal identity a student develops through study (D:EAU, p. 187).
- marking-criteria: the published standards against which work is assessed (D:EAU, p. 188).
- in-text-reference: a short reference in the text marking each use of a source (D:EAU, pp. 189-191).
- author-date: the referencing style that cites author and year in the text, usually called Harvard in UK institutions (D:EAU, p. 189).
- bibliography: a list that may include sources read but not cited, unlike a references list, though in some disciplines the two mean the same (D:EAU, p. 196).
- doi: a Digital Object Identifier, a persistent identifier for an online source, given in its reference (D:EAU, p. 197).
- et-al: the abbreviation for 'and others', used in place of further authors' names (D:EAU, pp. 190-191).

### A5a How practitioners apply it
<!-- brief: From the practitioner's side: the steps they take, the tools and artefacts they use, a worked example, and the decisions they make along the way. Separate what the authors prescribe (source) from how it is done in practice (reported, with sources) and what follows from it (editorial). -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
Practitioners here are tutors, academic skills staff, librarians and authors of learning material.

**What the authors prescribe** (source):
- Teach the values through examples of conduct in study, then ask how each would apply in a professional setting (D:EAU, pp. 186-187).
- Teach one style in full (the guide uses author-date) and send learners to their handbook or tutor for the style they need (D:EAU, pp. 188-189).
- Show each in-text form with a worked example: the reference in brackets before the full stop, or the author named in the sentence (D:EAU, pp. 189-191).
- Teach paraphrase as three steps on a real extract (D:EAU, pp. 194-195).
- Send learners to a referencing guide, internal or published, for the format of each source type (D:EAU, p. 198).

**How it is done in practice** (reported):
- Work is submitted through originality checking software, whose score is not proof of plagiarism but prompts a closer look (D:EAU, p. 194; evidence: reported).
- Institutions commit to engaging and empowering students on integrity as part of a whole-community approach (D:QAACHARTER; read by summary; evidence: reported).

**Worked example**:
At Introductory, the learner references three source types in author-date style and checks every in-text reference against the list. At Intermediate, they cut the quotations in a draft to one, paraphrase the rest in three steps and see how the references list now shows the scope of their reading. At Advanced, they discuss a case in which a group member has pasted unreferenced text, and decide what honesty, responsibility and courage ask of them (editorial).

### A5c Contexts and adaptations
<!-- brief: Where it is used (levels, sectors, practices, settings, modes such as online), and how it has been adapted for them, each with its source. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- Any discipline; the style changes by discipline (MHRA in the humanities, APA in psychology, Vancouver in medicine, IEEE in engineering, OSCOLA in law), never the demand (D:EAU, pp. 188-189).
- Academic integrity values: the International Center for Academic Integrity's Fundamental Values (third edition, 2021) name honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and courage, as informing ethical decisions and behaviour (D:ICAI; read by summary).
- UK sector commitment: QAA's Academic Integrity Charter (2020) commits providers to a whole-community approach against misconduct, including essay mills, plagiarism and collusion, and to engaging students and staff (D:QAACHARTER; read by summary).
- Information literacy: SCONUL's Seven Pillars (2011) places organising information effectively and ethically in its Manage pillar; recall, not read, has citing and referencing styles and the rights of others there too (D:SCONUL; read by summary; W-0016).
- Research beyond study: the Concordat to Support Research Integrity (2019, revised 2025) sets honesty, rigour, transparency and open communication, care and respect, and accountability (D:CONCORDAT; read by summary).

### A5d Misapplications
<!-- brief: Common ways it is misused or misread, the evidence for each, and how to avoid it. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: primary -->
- Treating referencing only as a rule against plagiarism, missing its use for showing scope and meeting marking criteria (D:EAU, pp. 188, 191-193; evidence: claimed); teach both purposes.
- Pasting text to reference later (D:EAU, p. 192; evidence: claimed); reference at the moment of use.
- Copying references from a discovery service without checking them (D:EAU, pp. 190-191; evidence: claimed).
- Mixing styles, or variations of one style, in a single piece of work (D:EAU, pp. 188-189; evidence: claimed).
- Quoting where a paraphrase would show understanding (D:EAU, pp. 193-194; evidence: claimed).
- Reading an originality score as a verdict (D:EAU, p. 194; evidence: claimed).
- (editorial) Treating patchwriting, close paraphrase that keeps the source's structure, only as misconduct, when it is often a stage in learning to write from sources (D:HOWARD; recall, not read; W-0028).

### A7a Criticisms of the theory and its application
<!-- brief: Criticisms of the theory itself and of how practitioners apply it, with the evidence for each, marked measured, reported or claimed. -->
<!-- label: both; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
- none-stated: no published criticism of this pathway exists.
- (editorial) The pathway reverses the guide's order. The guide frames referencing through integrity; the pathway teaches the mechanics first, so the reasons may arrive after the rules.
- (editorial) The guide grounds the recording of sources in part: accurate referencing as an example of honesty (p. 186), the details each reference needs (pp. 196-198), working efficiently against unintentional plagiarism (p. 192) and keeping a record while making notes (p. 49). It does not set out a method for recording and organising sources as they are found, which the information literacy models put with citing (A5c); SCONUL's Manage pillar supplies it (Part E, item 3).
- (editorial) The integrity literature argues that plagiarism by novice writers is often developmental rather than dishonest (Howard's patchwriting); the pathway's Advanced distinction between intentional and unintentional plagiarism points that way, its Introductory "plagiarism awareness" less so.

### A8 Selection
<!-- brief: When to choose it over named alternatives, and what it suits badly. Our reading (editorial). -->
<!-- label: editorial; status: drafted; basis: secondary -->
Choose this pathway when the aim is to acknowledge sources accurately, use them without plagiarism, or act on integrity values. For finding and evaluating the sources, choose `path:finding-using-sources`, which hands over at the point a source is recorded; for using sources actively in an argument, `path:academic-writing` and `path:criticality`; for ethics in research with participants and data, `path:researching`; for integrity questions raised by AI tools, `path:ai-technology`. It suits badly a learner whose need is copyright or licensing in publishing or professional work, which it touches only through the values.
