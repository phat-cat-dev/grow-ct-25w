## 3a. Tier statements and the kinds of knowledge

A loosely level-based set of statements about the practice at each tier, read through Anderson and Krathwohl's knowledge dimension (`ing:doc:bloom-anderson-krathwohl-2001`, 738db9b93165): factual knowledge (the terms and details), conceptual knowledge (how they fit together: models, principles), procedural knowledge (how to do it, and the criteria for choosing which method) and metacognitive knowledge (knowledge of cognition and of one's own), on a continuum from concrete to abstract. Factual and conceptual together are what Biggs and Marzano call declarative knowledge, knowing that; Biggs calls knowledge put to work functioning knowledge (`ing:doc:constructive-alignment-biggs-1996`); Marzano and Kendall place metacognition and the self-system above the cognitive system (`ing:doc:new-taxonomy-marzano-kendall-2007`, 988e7f88bf23). The statements are ing's, built up from the cards and the knowledge models; the classification is ing's reading. Section 4a gives the pathway model's own tier text, from the other direction (D-ing-0059).

**How the learner moves to mastery.** Two ladders run together here. On Anderson and Krathwohl's knowledge dimension the movement is from knowing what an academic practitioner is, through procedures for recording and planning one's development, to metacognitive knowledge of oneself; on Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia's affective domain it is from receiving and responding to one's values, through valuing and organising them, to characterisation, values so internalised that they mark the person. In Lave and Wenger's terms the student moves from the edge of an academic community towards its centre and changes who they are; in Marzano and Kendall's, the self-system, which decides whether one engages at all, becomes the object of thought. Mastery is the reflexive point at which the practitioner can examine and deconstruct themselves (N-0137), the top of every pathway's A11 ladder made the subject of a pathway of its own.

> **Source:** the guide's thread of graduate identity (Chapters 1, 3, 10 and 14) and its account of learning as a journey from the edge of a discourse community to its centre; reflexivity and the affective domain from outside the guide. Discrete; overlaps Starting, Progressing and Succeeding (disposition), Reflective Practice (method) and Referencing (values) by design.
>
> **Introductory: becoming aware of oneself as an academic practitioner.**
> What an academic practitioner is; academic practice and graduate identity; keeping a record of one's own learning; naming one's strengths, interests, goals and values. Goal: see oneself as someone who practises, and notice what matters to oneself in study.
>
> **Intermediate: developing an identity within an academic community.**
> How communities shape identity: participation from the edge towards the centre, conventions, integrity as a community's values; planning one's development against one's own values and goals; noticing how one's values, beliefs and background shape learning and the response to feedback. Goal: develop deliberately, aware of oneself.
>
> **Advanced: owning, articulating and examining one's identity and values.**
> Articulating one's identity and values with evidence; acting from an organised set of values when they conflict; reflexivity: examining and deconstructing one's own identity, assumptions and position as a knower. Goal: an academic practitioner who knows, owns and questions who they are.
>
> **Progression logic:** aware of oneself → developing within a community → owning and examining oneself.
