# Presenting: the cards in context

D-ing-0055 keeps each card in the core as the pathway template defines it and puts here what lies beyond the template: each card's vocabulary, the book topics under it, the benchmark evidence, the crosswalk to other frameworks, the pedagogy beneath it and how other authors describe it, then the vocabulary across the cards, the tiers from two directions and a table of correspondences. This file speaks to the core; it is not part of it. Its shape is the golden template (`ing-cards-template.md`, D-ing-0061).

Sources: the cards and their theory and frameworks from the core's A10; vocabulary from `ing-card-vocabulary.json`; book topics from adp-0212's draft book map (`mta-nts-ho-adp-nts-0007`), every quote found in the guide's Markdown copy by script and its page recomputed from the copy's page markers; benchmark evidence from adp-0212's card drafts (counts are passages / texts, of 54), each extract read by ing; other authors from META's INGEST runs in UNI's `library/ingested/theory/`, each with its SHA-256 prefix, given from the run's agent definition, never quoting the author. How the benchmark evidence is read: **skill** where the passage describes the learner doing the capability; **theme** where the practice appears only as an item in a list of attributes, methods or assessment types.

## 3a. Tier statements and the kinds of knowledge

A loosely level-based set of statements about the practice at each tier, read through Anderson and Krathwohl's knowledge dimension (`ing:doc:bloom-anderson-krathwohl-2001`, 738db9b93165): factual knowledge (the terms and details), conceptual knowledge (how they fit together: models, principles), procedural knowledge (how to do it, and the criteria for choosing which method) and metacognitive knowledge (knowledge of cognition and of one's own), on a continuum from concrete to abstract. Factual and conceptual together are what Biggs and Marzano call declarative knowledge, knowing that; Biggs calls knowledge put to work functioning knowledge (`ing:doc:constructive-alignment-biggs-1996`); Marzano and Kendall place metacognition and the self-system above the cognitive system (`ing:doc:new-taxonomy-marzano-kendall-2007`, 988e7f88bf23). The statements are ing's, built up from the cards and the knowledge models; the classification is ing's reading. Section 4a gives the pathway model's own tier text, from the other direction (D-ing-0059).

| Tier | Statement about the practice | Knowledge type | Cognitive process | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Introductory | Explains what a presentation tests, as assessment and as a skill employers look for. | conceptual | understand |  |

**How the learner moves to mastery.** Across the tiers the weight moves along Anderson and Krathwohl's continuum: Introductory is mostly declarative and procedural, the recipe for a presentation, with a first check of one's own performance in practice; Intermediate is procedural, design and delivery across formats, with the self-system entering as the learner manages their own nerves; Advanced is metacognitive, the learner presenting in professional forms, working in a team and defending an argument as someone with standing in their field. In Marzano and Kendall's terms the self-system, beliefs about oneself and one's emotional response, is unusually present in this pathway from the start. Mastery is presenting and collaborating as a professional, the map's Advanced goal, which is the core's A11 ladder from compliance to flow.

**The map's text (Pathway 7, Presenting)**

> **Source:** Chapter 11. Discrete; group-work material folds in at the advanced tier (may spin out later — see flag).
>
> **Introductory — what a presentation is, and how one is put together.**
> The presentation as both assessment and transferable skill (often tested at interview) — including a brief nod to the different types (such as individual, poster, group, professional, and others; covered fully at intermediate). Reading the brief carefully. The five steps (read instructions → research → plan → design visual aids → practise). Structure: a hook, a main body with one point per slide, a conclusion returning to the objectives. Goal: understand the basic recipe for building a presentation.
>
> **Intermediate — delivering it well, across different formats.**
> The types in full (individual, poster, group, professional/practice-based, interview-based, technology-based) and their differing expectations. Visual-aid design: "less is more," and accessibility (colour, font, legibility). Managing nerves and avoiding the classic errors (Natalie's and Nasser's failures). Goal: create and deliver a competent, coherent presentation, adapted to its format.
>
> **Advanced — presenting with authority, and collaborating.**
> Developing academic voice; asserting and defending an argument under questioning. Returns to three specific formats from the intermediate types list — the professional pitch, conference poster, viva-style interview — for depth at a harder level. And **working in groups**: collaborative assessment, personality types (OCEAN), leadership and conflict resolution (the Ross example), the reflective element tutors assess. Goal: presenting and collaborating as a professional.
>
> **Note (remapped 3 Sep 2026):** original mapping placed types at introductory and the five-step/structure content at intermediate; reversed after review — the mechanics of building a presentation are the more fundamental introductory skill, and format-specific handling is better placed once a learner already has one built. Advanced's return to three formats is a deliberate depth-pass on a subset of intermediate's list, not a reintroduction.
>
> **Flags:** group work is genuinely distinct and could spin out into its own pathway later. This is also one of the less writing-centric pathways — pressure-test before building (CLAUDE.md guardrail).

The map's voice-band anchor governs the lesson register and is left out here.

**Where the two directions differ** (ing's reading, recorded, not resolved): the two agree on the order (the map's 3 September remap): the five steps and structure at Introductory, the types, visual aids and nerves at Intermediate, professional formats, academic voice and group work at Advanced. i1 and i3 add the map's own Introductory items (assessment and transferable skill; reading the brief) and the guide's fifth step (practising) as cards.
