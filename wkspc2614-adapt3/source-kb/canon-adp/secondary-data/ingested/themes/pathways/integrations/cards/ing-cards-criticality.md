# Criticality: the cards in context

D-ing-0055 keeps each card in the core as the pathway template defines it and puts here what lies beyond the template: each card's vocabulary, the book topics under it, the benchmark evidence, the crosswalk to other frameworks, the pedagogy beneath it and how other authors describe it, then the vocabulary across the cards, the tiers from two directions and a table of correspondences. This file speaks to the core; it is not part of it. Its shape is the golden template (`ing-cards-template.md`, D-ing-0061).

Sources: the cards and their theory and frameworks from the core's A10; vocabulary from `ing-card-vocabulary.json`; book topics from adp-0212's draft book map (`mta-nts-ho-adp-nts-0007`), every quote found in the guide's Markdown copy by script and its page recomputed from the copy's page markers; benchmark evidence from adp-0212's card drafts (counts are passages / texts, of 54), each extract read by ing; other authors from META's INGEST runs in UNI's `library/ingested/theory/`, each with its SHA-256 prefix, given from the run's agent definition, never quoting the author. Quotations from the guide stay within 25 words.

How the benchmark evidence is read: **skill** where the passage describes the learner doing the capability; **theme** where the practice appears only as an item in a list of attributes, methods or assessment types.

## 3a. Tier statements and the kinds of knowledge

A loosely level-based set of statements about the practice at each tier, read through Anderson and Krathwohl's knowledge dimension (`ing:doc:bloom-anderson-krathwohl-2001`, 738db9b93165): factual knowledge (the terms and details), conceptual knowledge (how they fit together: models, principles), procedural knowledge (how to do it, and the criteria for choosing which method) and metacognitive knowledge (knowledge of cognition and of one's own), on a continuum from concrete to abstract. Factual and conceptual together are what Biggs and Marzano call declarative knowledge, knowing that; Biggs calls knowledge put to work functioning knowledge (`ing:doc:constructive-alignment-biggs-1996`); Marzano and Kendall place metacognition and the self-system above the cognitive system (`ing:doc:new-taxonomy-marzano-kendall-2007`, 988e7f88bf23). The statements are ing's, built up from the cards and the knowledge models; the classification is ing's reading. Section 4a gives the pathway model's own tier text, from the other direction (D-ing-0059).

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Intermediate | Tells analysis from description, and evaluation from analysis, and recognises evaluative steers ("To what extent", "How far", "Assess"). | conceptual | analyse | the concepts of the critical skills enter |

**How the learner moves to mastery.** Across the tiers the weight moves along Anderson and Krathwohl's continuum: Introductory is mostly declarative, what a discourse is and what being critical means, with one procedure (questioning one's own choices) and a first noticing of one's own critical thinking; Intermediate is mostly procedural, analysis and evaluation used on real sources, with strategic knowledge of how to read beginning; Advanced is mostly metacognitive and creative, the learner judging, synthesising and writing from a position they know to be theirs. In Biggs's terms the movement is from knowing about criticality to using it; in Barnett's, from critical skills towards reflexivity; in Marzano and Kendall's, from the cognitive system to metacognition and the self-system. Mastery is criticality as the guide frames it, a mindset rather than a set of skills, which is the core's A11 ladder from compliance to flow.

> The book's central reframe: "being critical" is *not* a switch you flick on for an assessment, nor a tidy set of skills — it's a mindset. Being critical begins at the research stage (John's search example shows critical thought in the *choices* he makes). Mapping the discourse: scope, key works, influential authors, themes, contentious areas. The link to marking criteria. Goal: understand criticality as a habit of mind.
>
> **Intermediate — the critical skills in action.**
> Analysis: breaking an interpretation open (the Higgins example — lifting the bonnet rather than describing the car). Evaluation: assessing the *relative* value of factors, and recognising evaluative steers ("To what extent…", "How far…", "Assess…"). Engaging with journal articles strategically: the structural map (abstract / introduction / methodology / results / discussion / conclusion) and savvy, non-linear reading. Active versus passive engagement (the Susie-versus-John contrast). Goal: analyse and evaluate sources critically.
>
> **Advanced — articulating criticality.**
> Forming judgements ("X is more important than Y *because*…"); synthesis (controlling and drawing interpretations together in service of your argument); the **language of criticality** (however, therefore, furthermore, moreover, although…); discipline-specific vocabulary; developing the "critical edge." Goal: express critical thought with authority. This tier interlocks with Academic Writing.
>
> **Progression logic:** criticality as mindset → analysing and evaluating → articulating judgement and synthesis.
> **Cross-links:** the advanced tier bridges into Academic Writing; the "writing critically" material is shared between the two.

The map's voice-band anchor governs the lesson register and is left out here.

**Where the two directions differ** (ing's reading, recorded, not resolved): the map places active against passive engagement at Intermediate; here the active approach (leading with one's own point) is part of e3 at Advanced, and d3 carries only the strategic reading. Otherwise the two agree: the mindset, the research stage and mapping the discourse at Introductory; analysis, evaluation, evaluative steers and the journal article at Intermediate; judgement, synthesis, the language of criticality and the critical edge at Advanced.
