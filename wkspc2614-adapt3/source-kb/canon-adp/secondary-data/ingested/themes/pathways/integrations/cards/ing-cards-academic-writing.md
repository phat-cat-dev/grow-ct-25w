## 3a. Tier statements and the kinds of knowledge

A loosely level-based set of statements about the practice at each tier, read through Anderson and Krathwohl's knowledge dimension (`ing:doc:bloom-anderson-krathwohl-2001`, 738db9b93165): factual knowledge (the terms and details), conceptual knowledge (how they fit together: models, principles), procedural knowledge (how to do it, and the criteria for choosing which method) and metacognitive knowledge (knowledge of cognition and of one's own), on a continuum from concrete to abstract. Factual and conceptual together are what Biggs and Marzano call declarative knowledge, knowing that; Biggs calls knowledge put to work functioning knowledge (`ing:doc:constructive-alignment-biggs-1996`); Marzano and Kendall place metacognition and the self-system above the cognitive system (`ing:doc:new-taxonomy-marzano-kendall-2007`, 988e7f88bf23). The statements are ing's, built up from the cards and the knowledge models; the classification is ing's reading. Section 4a gives the pathway model's own tier text, from the other direction (D-ing-0059).

| Tier | Statement about the practice | Knowledge type | Cognitive process | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Introductory | Names the stages of the writing cycle and the parts of an essay (introduction, body, conclusion), and the common errors (comma splice, over-long sentences, apostrophes, colon and semicolon). | factual | remember |  |
| Introductory | Explains what a question's steer and keywords ask for, and why the three false assumptions about academic writing are false. | conceptual | understand |  |
| Introductory | Plans and gets a first draft down, then proofreads it. | procedural (one method) | apply | declarative knowledge first put to work, with a set cycle |
| Introductory | Spots the errors they tend to make in their own drafts. | metacognitive (beginning) | understand | knowledge of one's own writing appears, prompted |
| Intermediate | Explains macro and micro structure, the judgement-bearing final sentence and how the essay and reflective writing differ as genres. | conceptual | analyse | the concepts of structure and genre enter |
| Intermediate | Edits for clarity and precision (SVO, active voice, cutting non-essential text) and integrates sources by the voices technique. | procedural | apply |  |
| Intermediate | Reviews their own draft by the four pillars and decides what to change. | metacognitive (strategic) | evaluate | knowledge of one's own writing, used to change it |

| Advanced | Uses the language of criticality to give writing a critical edge, leading with their own point. | procedural (criteria for using sources) | create |  |
| Advanced | Writes as a member of their discipline, aware of its conventions and of their own voice within them. | metacognitive (self-knowledge) | evaluate | functioning knowledge: writing owned within the discipline |

**How the learner moves to mastery.** Across the tiers the weight moves along Anderson and Krathwohl's continuum: Introductory is mostly declarative and procedural, the cycle, the parts of an essay and the common errors, with a first noticing of one's own errors; Intermediate is mostly procedural, editing and structuring and integrating sources, with the four pillars turned on one's own draft; Advanced is conceptual and metacognitive, the conventions of a discipline read and then written from within. In Biggs's terms the movement is from knowing how to write to writing that does its work in a discipline; in Lea and Street's, from study skills through academic socialisation to academic literacies. Mastery is disciplinary self-awareness in writing, the map's Advanced goal, which is the core's A11 ladder from compliance to flow.

**The map's text (Pathway 6, Academic Writing)**

> **Source:** Chapter 9 (minus the research-document genres, which move to Pathway 9), plus the "writing critically" material from Chapter 8. Discrete. **Pilot candidate.**
>
> **Introductory — getting a draft down, and getting the basics right.**
> Demystification first: the three false assumptions (that academic writing is a special genre, wholly different, intellectually loftier — all false). The **writing cycle** (understand the task → plan → first draft → edit → proofread). Emma's blank-page freeze and "better a draft than nothing." Understanding the task: the *steer* and the *keywords*. Basic essay structure (introduction / body / conclusion). The common-errors list (comma splice, over-long sentences, apostrophes, colon/semicolon). Goal: produce a competent piece without panicking.
>
> **Intermediate — writing with argument, structure, and sources.**
> Clarity and precision: the SVO model, active over passive voice, cutting non-essential text, the unedited-versus-edited paragraph. Macro and micro structure: topic sentences, the judgement-bearing final sentence, paragraph arrangement. Integrating sources actively via the "voices" technique. The four pillars as a self-review lens. Genre awareness: the essay in depth, and reflective writing. Goal: an argued, well-evidenced piece.
>
> **Advanced — writing within your discipline.**
> There is no monolithic "academic writing" — the three discipline exemplars (chemistry/sciences, education/social sciences, history/humanities) and reading for their conventions of style, evidence, vocabulary, and discourse. Developing a critical edge — the language of criticality applied in writing. Goal: disciplinary self-awareness in writing.
>
> **Note (principles over literalism):** the book presents discipline conventions *first* in Chapter 9; this places them at the *advanced* tier deliberately, because a beginner still fighting the blank page can't yet read for disciplinary convention. Confirm at the pilot pressure-test.
> **Research-genre hand-off:** the research proposal, literature review, and report — which Chapter 9 includes — are **taught in Researching (Pathway 9)**, not here, since they're the outputs of doing research. Writing keeps discipline conventions and the essay/reflective genres.
> **Cross-links:** shares "writing critically" with Criticality; draws on Finding & Using Sources and Referencing.

The map's voice-band anchor governs the lesson register and is left out here.

**Where the two directions differ** (ing's reading, recorded, not resolved): the map puts the voices technique, and the four pillars as a self-review lens, at Intermediate; e2 (integrating sources actively) sits at Advanced here with the critical edge, and the four pillars are at the top of Intermediate (d3), as in the map. The map places discipline conventions at Advanced deliberately, against the book's order (its own note); the cards follow the map.
