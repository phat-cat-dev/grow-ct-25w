# Pathway Tier Mapping

**Programme:** Academic Practice e-learning
**Source:** *Excelling at University* (Dennis & Abbott, Routledge, 2026)
**Status:** Draft v3 — all thirteen pathways mapped (incl. authored Track B), for SME review
**Purpose:** Defines the introductory → intermediate → advanced progression for each pathway, so that later authoring has a settled spine to work from.

---

## How to read this document

This maps each pathway across the three proficiency bands agreed in `open-decisions.md`: **introductory, intermediate, advanced**, defined by *complexity and prior experience with the practice*, not by credit level. A student may sit in introductory for one practice and advanced for another; the bands describe experience with the practice, not year of study.

The mapping is **grounded in the book but not bound to its chapter order**. The book is written to be *read* (concepts first, practice second); a *learning* progression sometimes inverts that, because a beginner can't yet do the sophisticated thing a chapter opens with. Where the tiering departs from the book's sequence, it's flagged. This principle — the book's pedagogy underpins the tiers, but the tiers serve the learner — matters even more at the content-writing stage than here.

This document records design decisions. It does **not** need to be written in the student voice; the Voice Specification governs lesson copy, not this planning artefact.

A note on the book's method that runs through every pathway: the book teaches almost entirely through a **recurring cast** — Natalie (disorganised, performance-motivated) contrasted with Jess (self-regulating, mastery-motivated), plus John (criticality), Emma (the blank page), Will and Ross (reflection and group work). These contrasts are the book's core pedagogical device and are reusable as worked examples across tiers.

## Pathway 1 — Starting, Progressing & Succeeding

**Source:** Chapters 2 and 14. A full tiered pathway **and the programme's entry point** — surfaced first, it introduces the others and carries the orientation tone. This is the *dispositional* layer: who you're becoming and why you're motivated, distinct from the mechanics of learning (Pathway 2).
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default). Confirmed at tier-spec v1.0.

**Introductory — Starting.**
Finding your feet. What "excelling" actually means; the shift from school/college to university and why it feels different; growth versus fixed mindset; understanding your own motivation (mastery versus performance); setting goals and breaking them into achievable sub-goals; building self-efficacy (belief in your own capability).

**Intermediate — Progressing.**
Keeping going. Sustaining motivation across a whole course; handling setbacks, disappointment, and anxiety constructively; setting realistic expectations without tipping into paralysing perfectionism; knowing when and how to seek support — early rather than late.

**Advanced — Succeeding.**
Becoming a graduate. Independence and self-direction; consolidating a graduate identity; the transition out of university into professional life; becoming a lifelong learner who carries these dispositions into work.

**Progression logic:** find your feet → sustain and adapt → become a graduate.
**Cross-links:** every other pathway leans on this for the motivational/dispositional layer; Pathway 2 (self-regulated learning) is its mechanical counterpart.

## Pathway 2 — Understanding Learning, Assessment & Feedback

**Source:** Chapters 3, 5, 6, plus the self-regulated-learning mechanics from Chapter 4. Grouped because the three are mutually reinforcing: you can't understand feedback without assessment, or either without how learning is designed. This is the *mechanics* of managing your own learning — the "how", where Pathway 1 is the "why/who".
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default).

**Introductory — how learning at university works.**
The orientation tier. What learning *is* here: the discourse community and the journey from its periphery (novice) to its centre (expert); deep versus surface learning; sense-making and building knowledge structures; active versus passive learning; tutors as facilitators. Then what *assessment* is, taken broadly (not just exams), and what *feedback* is (comments plus a mark, found everywhere). The learning framework: module handbook, lectures versus tutorials, synchronous versus asynchronous, formative versus summative. Studying-technique basics (active note-making, engaging rather than transcribing) fold in.

**Intermediate — working with the system, and managing your own learning.**
Why the system is built as it is, and how to drive your own learning within it. Constructivism and behaviourism (why activities are designed as they are); intended learning outcomes and constructive alignment; the formative-to-summative strategy. Reading marking criteria (Table 5.1 — what "developed" versus "detailed" versus "basic" means across mark bands). The **feedback cycle** (Review → Interpret → Articulate → Apply) and turning feedback into an action plan. **Self-regulated learning mechanics**: the three phases (forethought → behaviour → evaluation), goal-setting, and monitoring and adapting your strategy mid-task (the Natalie/Jess and Leon examples). Mindset's link to feedback (cross-reference Pathway 1 for the motivation piece rather than re-teaching it).

**Advanced — owning your development within the system.**
Applying marking criteria to your *own* work (self-assessment, modelled by the radon-gas exemplar); self- and peer-assessment; using feedback diagnostically to open a dialogue with tutors; assessment agility (recognising nuanced differences between genres); using assessment strategically across a whole degree; synthesising feedback over time into sustained development.

**Progression logic:** understand the system → work with it and self-regulate → own your development.
**Cross-links:** Assessment and Feedback are one pathway by design; the self-regulation mechanics here are the operational counterpart to Pathway 1's dispositional material; Reflective Practice (Pathway 10) is the engine beneath the feedback cycle and self-assessment.

## Pathway 3 — Finding & Using Sources

**Source:** Chapter 7. Discrete. This is searching and evaluating *sources* — distinct from conceiving a research *project* (Pathway 9).
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default).

**Introductory — searching as a discipline.**
The reframe first: academic searching is not looking up trivia — it's exploratory, iterative, and effortful, "a science." Why research matters; the library discovery service as first port of call; using the module reading list; the five-stage shape (plan → keywords → discovery service → evaluate → extend). The Natalie-versus-Jess search contrast anchors disorganised versus planned searching. Goal: reliably find sources using the discovery service.

**Intermediate — search craft.**
Planning by first reflecting on what you already know, to target gaps; identifying keywords and synonyms; Boolean operators (AND / OR / NOT / " " / ( ) / \*); limiters (date, subject, source type, availability); iterative cycles of narrowing. Goal: targeted, efficient searches that return the *right* results.

**Advanced — extending and evaluating deeply.**
Evaluating sources with discernment: source-type expectations; currency, authority, and — most importantly — relevance to *your own* knowledge-building. Extending beyond the discovery service: subject databases, citation searching, Google Scholar and Books, and the comprehensive searching a dissertation needs. The book frames "extending your search" as a final-year/dissertation activity, so this is a natural advanced tier. Goal: comprehensive, discerning engagement with the discourse.

**Progression logic:** find sources → find the *right* sources efficiently → engage comprehensively and critically.
**Cross-links:** feeds Criticality (evaluating sources), Academic Writing (integrating them), and Researching (the search phase of a project).

## Pathway 4 — Referencing, Citation & Academic Integrity

**Source:** Chapter 10. Grouped, and taught **in that order**: referencing → citation → integrity.
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default).

**Introductory — referencing.**
The concrete, doable skill first. Why we reference; what references look like (in-text reference versus references list); the mechanics of author-date/Harvard for common source types (books, journal articles, web pages); accuracy and consistency (and why copy-pasting references introduces errors). *Basic plagiarism awareness* threads here too — enough to stay safe — even though the full values framework sits at advanced. Goal: reference correctly.

**Intermediate — citation.**
Citation as a purposeful tool, not a compliance chore. In-text citation as evidence — of the *scope* of your research (the references list is what many tutors read first), of critical engagement (references signpost where you're using sources to build an argument), and of disciplinary knowledge. Quotation versus paraphrase, and why paraphrasing is the more skilful and valuable act. Handling trickier sources. Goal: cite purposefully and paraphrase well.

**Advanced — academic integrity.**
The values framework underneath everything: the six ICAI values (honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility, courage) and what each means in practice; plagiarism (intentional and unintentional) and how efficient working prevents it; integrity as a component of graduate identity carried into professional life. Goal: integrity as scholarly and professional practice.

**Note on ordering (principles over literalism):** Chapter 10 opens with the integrity *values* then covers referencing. This pathway reverses that — start with the concrete skill (referencing), build to the ethical framework (integrity) as the culminating "why."
**Cross-links:** referencing supports Academic Writing; research ethics-in-practice (consent, data handling) is shared with Researching (Pathway 9) — integrity covers the values and plagiarism, Researching covers ethics in the field; integrity connects to the graduate-identity thread in Pathway 1.

## Pathway 5 — Criticality

**Source:** Chapter 8. Discrete; overlaps Academic Writing by design. Critical thinking is introduced up front within this pathway.
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default).

**Introductory — criticality as a mindset.**
The book's central reframe: "being critical" is *not* a switch you flick on for an assessment, nor a tidy set of skills — it's a mindset. Being critical begins at the research stage (John's search example shows critical thought in the *choices* he makes). Mapping the discourse: scope, key works, influential authors, themes, contentious areas. The link to marking criteria. Goal: understand criticality as a habit of mind.

**Intermediate — the critical skills in action.**
Analysis: breaking an interpretation open (the Higgins example — lifting the bonnet rather than describing the car). Evaluation: assessing the *relative* value of factors, and recognising evaluative steers ("To what extent…", "How far…", "Assess…"). Engaging with journal articles strategically: the structural map (abstract / introduction / methodology / results / discussion / conclusion) and savvy, non-linear reading. Active versus passive engagement (the Susie-versus-John contrast). Goal: analyse and evaluate sources critically.

**Advanced — articulating criticality.**
Forming judgements ("X is more important than Y *because*…"); synthesis (controlling and drawing interpretations together in service of your argument); the **language of criticality** (however, therefore, furthermore, moreover, although…); discipline-specific vocabulary; developing the "critical edge." Goal: express critical thought with authority. This tier interlocks with Academic Writing.

**Progression logic:** criticality as mindset → analysing and evaluating → articulating judgement and synthesis.
**Cross-links:** the advanced tier bridges into Academic Writing; the "writing critically" material is shared between the two.

## Pathway 6 — Academic Writing

**Source:** Chapter 9 (minus the research-document genres, which move to Pathway 9), plus the "writing critically" material from Chapter 8. Discrete. **Pilot candidate.**
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default). Confirmed: validated against L01/L02 at tier-spec STEP 5.

**Introductory — getting a draft down, and getting the basics right.**
Demystification first: the three false assumptions (that academic writing is a special genre, wholly different, intellectually loftier — all false). The **writing cycle** (understand the task → plan → first draft → edit → proofread). Emma's blank-page freeze and "better a draft than nothing." Understanding the task: the *steer* and the *keywords*. Basic essay structure (introduction / body / conclusion). The common-errors list (comma splice, over-long sentences, apostrophes, colon/semicolon). Goal: produce a competent piece without panicking.

**Intermediate — writing with argument, structure, and sources.**
Clarity and precision: the SVO model, active over passive voice, cutting non-essential text, the unedited-versus-edited paragraph. Macro and micro structure: topic sentences, the judgement-bearing final sentence, paragraph arrangement. Integrating sources actively via the "voices" technique. The four pillars as a self-review lens. Genre awareness: the essay in depth, and reflective writing. Goal: an argued, well-evidenced piece.

**Advanced — writing within your discipline.**
There is no monolithic "academic writing" — the three discipline exemplars (chemistry/sciences, education/social sciences, history/humanities) and reading for their conventions of style, evidence, vocabulary, and discourse. Developing a critical edge — the language of criticality applied in writing. Goal: disciplinary self-awareness in writing.

**Note (principles over literalism):** the book presents discipline conventions *first* in Chapter 9; this places them at the *advanced* tier deliberately, because a beginner still fighting the blank page can't yet read for disciplinary convention. Confirm at the pilot pressure-test.
**Research-genre hand-off:** the research proposal, literature review, and report — which Chapter 9 includes — are **taught in Researching (Pathway 9)**, not here, since they're the outputs of doing research. Writing keeps discipline conventions and the essay/reflective genres.
**Cross-links:** shares "writing critically" with Criticality; draws on Finding & Using Sources and Referencing.

## Pathway 7 — Presenting

**Source:** Chapter 11. Discrete; group-work material folds in at the advanced tier (may spin out later — see flag).
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default). ~~Provisional: confirm at pathway pressure-test.~~ **Confirmed 4 Sep 2026** at presenting-introductory A2 (f05d72d); bands 2–4 stand.

**Introductory — what a presentation is, and how one is put together.**
The presentation as both assessment and transferable skill (often tested at interview) — including a brief nod to the different types (such as individual, poster, group, professional, and others; covered fully at intermediate). Reading the brief carefully. The five steps (read instructions → research → plan → design visual aids → practise). Structure: a hook, a main body with one point per slide, a conclusion returning to the objectives. Goal: understand the basic recipe for building a presentation.

**Intermediate — delivering it well, across different formats.**
The types in full (individual, poster, group, professional/practice-based, interview-based, technology-based) and their differing expectations. Visual-aid design: "less is more," and accessibility (colour, font, legibility). Managing nerves and avoiding the classic errors (Natalie's and Nasser's failures). Goal: create and deliver a competent, coherent presentation, adapted to its format.

**Advanced — presenting with authority, and collaborating.**
Developing academic voice; asserting and defending an argument under questioning. Returns to three specific formats from the intermediate types list — the professional pitch, conference poster, viva-style interview — for depth at a harder level. And **working in groups**: collaborative assessment, personality types (OCEAN), leadership and conflict resolution (the Ross example), the reflective element tutors assess. Goal: presenting and collaborating as a professional.

**Note (remapped 3 Sep 2026):** original mapping placed types at introductory and the five-step/structure content at intermediate; reversed after review — the mechanics of building a presentation are the more fundamental introductory skill, and format-specific handling is better placed once a learner already has one built. Advanced's return to three formats is a deliberate depth-pass on a subset of intermediate's list, not a reintroduction.

**Flags:** group work is genuinely distinct and could spin out into its own pathway later. This is also one of the less writing-centric pathways — pressure-test before building (CLAUDE.md guardrail).

## Pathway 8 — Revising & Exams

**Source:** Chapter 13. Discrete.
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default). Provisional: confirm at pathway pressure-test.

**Introductory — understanding exams and the need for strategy.**
Exams as learning tools, not obstacles; the forms of exam (essay, multiple-choice, open-book, short-answer, technical) and their differing demands; understanding exam anxiety and its real causes (Natalie's last-minute panic); why passive revision fails. Goal: understand exams and why a strategy is essential.

**Intermediate — building a revision strategy.**
The five-stage strategy (time available → identify gaps → allocate time → choose techniques → monitor). Active versus passive learning applied to revision; time management; targeted revision; cognitive resilience. Goal: design and run a working revision strategy.

**Advanced — active techniques and exam-day performance.**
The full active-technique toolkit matched to how you learn — visual (mind maps, visual note-taking, flashcards, mnemonics, posters), auditory (reading aloud, recordings, discussion), kinaesthetic (spatial placing, role-play, movement, models); matching techniques to exam type; and exam-day strategy (the Jess-versus-Natalie contrast — arriving early, reading instructions, allocating and planning time per answer, writing to time). Goal: optimised, self-monitored performance under exam conditions.

**Flag:** another less writing-centric pathway — pressure-test before building (CLAUDE.md guardrail).

## Pathway 9 — Researching & Research Methods

**Source:** the research material the book scatters — the research proposal, literature review, and report (Chapter 9); research ethics and data handling (Chapter 10); the dissertation as the culminating integration of every literacy (Chapter 5); and the discourse-community framing (Chapter 3). This pathway pulls those pieces into one coherent progression. It is where the dissertation lives. **New in v2.**
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Intermediate, Intermediate→§16 Advanced, Advanced→§16 Masters (bands 3–5, up-skew). Rationale: this pathway's introductory tier already assumes a capable student (see this section's Notes).

**Introductory — what research is and how a project is shaped.**
Research as a structured project distinct from an essay; moving from a broad topic to a specific research question (the general-to-specific table in Chapter 9); the rationale ("why should this research exist?"); aims and objectives. Goal: frame a researchable question.

**Intermediate — the research-document genres.**
The research proposal (the full question set — title, rationale, aims, methodology, organisation, indicative bibliography — and the ultranationalism exemplar); the literature review as *critique* (mapping the discourse and synthesising, not describing); the report structure (sciences versus business variants). Goal: produce the documents that scaffold a research project.

**Advanced — running research with rigour and ethics.**
Methodology and approach (data, analysis, theoretical lenses); research ethics and the handling of data (the ESRC principles, consent, anonymisation, the ethics form) — drawn from Chapter 10; the dissertation as the culminating integration of every literacy. Goal: conduct independent research to a professional/scholarly standard.

**Progression logic:** frame a question → produce the research documents → run research with rigour and ethics.
**Notes:** this pathway is heavily final-year/postgraduate, so its tiers skew later than most — "introductory" here is already a fairly capable student, which is fine under a proficiency model. Research ethics is *shared* with Referencing/Integrity (Pathway 4): integrity covers values and plagiarism, Researching covers ethics-in-practice (consent, data) — they cross-reference rather than duplicate.
**Cross-links:** builds on Finding & Using Sources (the search phase), Criticality (the literature review), and Academic Writing (clear expression), which now hands the research genres to this pathway.

## Pathway 10 — Reflective Practice

**Source:** the reflection strand of Chapter 4, plus reflective models used across the book. Discrete. This is reflection as a *professional discipline* — formal, assessed, and career-long — distinct from the self-regulation mechanics folded into Pathway 2.
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default).

**Introductory — what reflection is and why it matters.**
Reflection as both a learner's tool and a professional practice; the simple ERA cycle (describe → reflect → take action); reflecting honestly (positives before negatives; no reflection is wholly positive or negative).

**Intermediate — structured reflection turned into action.**
Reflecting on real experiences (feedback, tasks, group work); avoiding pure description — reflecting *critically* on your own performance; turning reflection into concrete action plans.

**Advanced — reflection as professional practice.**
The fuller models (Gibbs, Kolb) used in assessed reflection in vocational fields (nursing, teaching, social work); reflection as continuing professional development; reflection as part of graduate identity.

**Progression logic:** what reflection is → structured, critical reflection → professional reflective practice.
**Cross-links:** the engine beneath the feedback cycle and self-assessment (Pathway 2), reflective writing (Pathway 6), the group-work reflective element (Pathway 7), and experiential learning (Pathway 11). The overlap with self-regulated learning (Pathway 2) is handled by cross-reference: SRL is reflection pointed inward at your learning process; Reflective Practice is reflection as an assessed professional discipline.

## Pathway 12 — AI & Technology (authored / Track B)

**Track B (authored).** The book is silent on this, so the content is authored to the Voice Specification from institutional and sector sources rather than converted from the book. Two consequences: sources are recorded below (provenance matters for sign-off in a way it doesn't for book-derived pathways), and **this mapping needs SME approval before authoring**, not just a pressure-test.

**Framing decision.** The spine is **AI literacy** — defined across the sector not as prompt fluency but as the ability to *understand, use, critically evaluate, and act responsibly* with AI. A recurring insight from the sources: AI literacy is measured by *how well you decide*, not how fluently you operate a tool — informed restraint and refusal ("no, not here, not yet") are valid literacy outcomes. Academic integrity is therefore **not** a final-tier bolt-on; it threads through all three tiers, matching how UNESCO treats ethics as cross-cutting.

**Sources:**
- University of Sheffield, *Using Generative AI* hub and sub-pages (sheffield.ac.uk): *GenAI and academic integrity* (the five misconduct risks — plagiarism, buying/commissioning, coursework sites, fabrication/"hallucination", collusion; the "golden rule" of checking assessment guidance first; the never-cite-GenAI rule); *How to use GenAI critically* (critical AI literacy as a socio-economic/cultural phenomenon, not just a technology; the pros/cons weighing habit; the five-stage workflow — background thinking → initial prompting → dialogic prompting → critical analysis/fact-checking → Acknowledge/Describe/Evidence disclosure); *for essays and reports* (GenAI as a "critical friend" for scoping, planning, translating, proofreading; four integrity self-checks; red flags — generic "AI speak", dropped hedging, American English); *for revision* (as an active-recall/self-testing aid, not a replacement for retrieval practice).
- Russell Group, *Principles on the use of generative AI tools in education* (2023): support students to become AI-literate (understand opportunities, limitations, ethics); adapt to ethical use; uphold rigour and integrity.
- UNESCO *AI Competency Framework for Students* (2024) and the Ng et al. AI-literacy model: the tiering backbone **Understand → Apply → Create**, with components *know & understand / use & apply / evaluate & create / AI ethics*, ethics cross-cutting.
- Cardiff Met AI Hub: institutional home; the student-facing hub is login-walled, so Cardiff-Met specifics (approved tools, local policy, local disclosure process) must be supplied by the SME at authoring and should replace the Sheffield specifics.
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default). Provisional: confirm at SME sign-off.

**Introductory — understand AI (AI literacy foundations).**
What generative AI is and, crucially, is not: how these tools work at a high level (large datasets, pattern generation) and their significant limitations — inaccuracy, fabrication/"hallucination", bias, privacy and data risks, non-reproducibility. Moving past the misconception that AI is a solution to everything. Foundational integrity threaded in: the "golden rule" (always check the module/assessment guidance and ask the tutor if unsure) and that GenAI outputs should never be cited. Goal: understand what AI is, what it can't be trusted to do, and the first rule of using it in study.

**Intermediate — use AI critically (apply it to study tasks).**
Using AI as a "critical friend" that augments your own thinking rather than replacing it. Anchored by a concrete, teachable **critical-use workflow**: (1) background thinking — establish what *you* already know and think first, and never just paste in the essay title; (2) initial prompting — build a prompt with a role, a function, and limitations; (3) dialogic prompting — treat it as a dialogue, pushing for counter-arguments and depth; (4) critical analysis and fact-checking — verify everything against reliable sources, checking for fabrication and bias; (5) Acknowledge, Describe, Evidence — disclose how AI was used. Applied across real tasks: essays and reports (scoping, planning/structuring, proofreading), revision (self-testing, active recall), and reading/note-taking — always using outputs to *inform* your own work, never copying and pasting. Critical evaluation of output: questioning accuracy, spotting fabricated references, recognising bias, noticing generic "AI speak" or dropped academic hedging. The pros/cons weighing habit: deciding, per task, whether AI is even the right tool (including its environmental cost). Informed restraint: recognising when *not* to use AI, and that over-reliance can erode your own voice, creativity, and language development. Goal: use AI critically and selectively, evaluating everything it produces.

**Advanced — evaluate, create with, and act ethically around AI.**
The most demanding tier: sophisticated critical evaluation and responsible action. Deep AI literacy — evaluating AI systems and outputs against academic standards; the wider issues (bias, equity of access, environmental impact, the human-centred mindset that keeps agency with the student). Academic integrity in full: the five misconduct risks and how to avoid them; full disclosure/acknowledgement where permitted; the distinction between permitted support and unfair advantage. Using AI responsibly in more complex creative/research work within an ethical framework. AI and graduate identity — being a discerning, responsible AI user in professional life (the Russell Group's "leaders in an AI-enabled world"). Goal: evaluate and use AI to a professional, ethical standard, and uphold integrity.

**Progression logic:** understand AI and its limits → use it critically and selectively → evaluate, create with, and act ethically around it. (Integrity is cross-cutting, not confined to the advanced tier.)
**Cross-links:** Referencing, Citation & Academic Integrity (Pathway 4) — the AI-integrity material is the AI-specific extension of that pathway's values and plagiarism content; cross-reference, don't duplicate. Criticality (Pathway 5) — critical evaluation of AI output is applied criticality. Academic Writing (Pathway 6) and Revising & Exams (Pathway 8) — the essays/reports and revision applications connect here. Finding & Using Sources (Pathway 3) — using AI to find information, and why AI output isn't a citable source.
**Notes:** the fastest-moving pathway in the programme — tools, policy, and sector guidance shift quickly, so build the content to be *updated*. The critical-use workflow and Acknowledge/Describe/Evidence model are Sheffield's; Cardiff Met's approved tools, local policy, and disclosure process must come from the SME. This pathway and Pathway 4 need coordination so they reinforce rather than repeat each other.

## Pathway 13 — Creativity (authored / Track B)

**Track B (authored).** As with AI, the content is authored from institutional and sector sources, provenance is recorded, and **the mapping needs SME approval before authoring**.

**Framing decision.** The institutional source reframes Creativity away from being an arts/enterprise topic and towards **a foundational academic skill across all disciplines** — creative problem-solving, idea generation, cognitive flexibility, and the synergy between creativity and critical thinking. Enterprise/entrepreneurship is *one downstream application*, not the spine. This matches the agreed steer and is how the tiers are built: creativity as a general academic capability first, enterprise as an advanced application.

**Sources:**
- Cardiff Met Library, *Creative Thinking at University* (library.cardiffmet.ac.uk/creativity): creativity as a developable, cross-disciplinary academic skill; creative problem-solving; idea generation and combinatorial creativity; cognitive flexibility; the creativity–critical-thinking synergy; overcoming barriers; discipline-specific sub-pages (business, art & design, health science, sport science, education, social policy, technology).
- QAA, *Enterprise and Entrepreneurship Education: Guidance for UK HE Providers* (2018): the enterprise strand's ready-made progression — the **awareness → mindset → capability → effectiveness** pipeline and the **learning "about" / "for" / "through"** distinction.
- Supporting concepts: De Bono (lateral thinking), Johnson (the "adjacent possible"), Bloom's taxonomy (create as highest-order), divergent/convergent thinking.
**Voice-band anchor:** Introductory→§16 Beginner, Intermediate→§16 Intermediate, Advanced→§16 Advanced (bands 2–4, default). Provisional: confirm at SME sign-off.

**Introductory — creativity as a foundational academic skill.**
Reframing creativity: not an innate artistic gift possessed by a few, but a developable skill that matters across every discipline. What creativity means in academic contexts — generating novel ideas, making unexpected connections, approaching problems from multiple angles. Divergent versus convergent thinking. The creativity–critical-thinking relationship (creativity generates ideas; critical thinking evaluates them — cross-references Pathway 5). Recognising and overcoming barriers: fear of failure, perfectionism, allowing "bad" ideas alongside good ones. Goal: understand creativity as a skill you can build, and start building it.

**Intermediate — creative methods in academic work.**
Applying creativity to real study tasks. Creative problem-solving: problem framing and reframing (seeing a problem from a new angle), generating multiple solutions before settling. Idea generation and innovation through synthesis — combinatorial creativity, the "adjacent possible" (innovation at the edges of what exists), interdisciplinary connection. Creative techniques applied to study: divergent-thinking methods (brainstorming, mind mapping, free writing), creative note-taking and reading, creative approaches to essays and revision. Cognitive flexibility and learning agility — adapting your approach rather than rigidly holding one strategy. Goal: use creative methods deliberately across academic tasks.

**Advanced — creativity as innovation, enterprise, and professional attribute.**
Creativity as a professional and value-creating capability. The enterprise/entrepreneurship strand enters here, built on the QAA pipeline: enterprise **awareness** (what it means and its relevance), entrepreneurial **mindset** (self-awareness, resilience, tolerance of ambiguity, turning ideas into action), entrepreneurial **capability** (identifying and developing opportunities), and **effectiveness** (self-directed value creation). The QAA "about / for / through" distinction maps onto how it's taught (learning *about* enterprise → *for* it → *through* practice). Creativity as a graduate/professional attribute (employer-valued; collaborative creativity in teams); creativity and resilience in navigating uncertainty. Goal: apply creativity to innovation, opportunity, and professional value creation.

**Progression logic:** creativity as a foundational skill → creative methods in study → creativity as innovation/enterprise/professional attribute.
**Cross-links:** Criticality (Pathway 5) — the creativity/critical-thinking synergy runs throughout; Reflective Practice (Pathway 10) — metacognitive awareness of your own creative process; Work-Based Learning (Pathway 11) and Starting/Progressing/Succeeding (Pathway 1) — the enterprise strand's mindset and effectiveness material connects to graduate identity and experiential learning.
