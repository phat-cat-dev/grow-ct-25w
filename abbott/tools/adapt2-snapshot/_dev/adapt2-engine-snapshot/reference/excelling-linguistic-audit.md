
# Excelling at University: Linguistic Audit
## Voice, Register, and Style Analysis for Content Generation
 
**Version:** v1.1, September 2026
**Source:** Dennis, C. and Abbott, S. (2026) *Excelling at University: Academic Literacies and the Development of Graduate Identity*. Routledge.
**Sample:** Chapters 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8 (approximately 15,000 words of running text analysed).
**Purpose:** Empirical foundation for voice-rules.json. All generation rules should be traceable to observations in this audit.
 
---
 
## 1. Sentence-level analysis
 
### 1.1 Sentence length and rhythm
 
The book uses a **varied sentence length pattern** with a distinctive rhythm: medium sentences (15–25 words) form the backbone, punctuated by short declarative sentences (5–12 words) for emphasis and occasional longer sentences (30–45 words) for qualification or multi-part claims.
 
**Observed pattern:** The rhythm typically runs: medium → medium → short (emphasis) → long (qualification) → medium. The short sentences almost always carry the key takeaway or a shift in direction.
 
**Examples from text:**
 
- Short for emphasis: "The point is that success isn't just about the marks you get for assessments." / "That's because notions of success are subjective." / "Critical thought is therefore not something you flick on when you start an assessment."
- Medium (backbone): "What you find will then inform the way you engage with the discourse focused more specifically on the use of Instagram."
- Long (qualification): "While it's certainly important to offer you practical guidance on effective practices related to studying, we also feel that to give you that extra bit of insight and understanding, that additional push needed to excel, you need to appreciate why you are expected to do things in a certain way."
**Rule for generation:** Vary sentence length. Average 18–22 words. Use short sentences (under 12 words) for key points and transitions. Permit longer sentences for qualified or multi-part claims but cap at approximately 45 words. Never use three or more long sentences consecutively.
 
### 1.2 Sentence openers
 
The book avoids repetitive sentence openers. Across a typical paragraph, no two consecutive sentences begin the same way.
 
**Observed opener types:**
- Subject-verb: "Success is often seen in terms of results."
- Conditional: "If you're new to studying at university, this book will give you a head start."
- Prepositional: "In keeping with its aspirational approach, we conceive of..."
- Temporal: "When beginning your research, therefore, your goal should be..."
- Contrast: "However, alternative perspectives have emerged."
- Demonstrative: "This is only a snapshot of John's initial research activity."
- Imperative: "Think back to that marking criteria."
- Participial: "Having thought carefully about the learning experience, she makes some notes."
**Rule for generation:** Vary sentence openers within each paragraph. No two consecutive sentences should begin with the same word or grammatical structure. Avoid starting more than one sentence per paragraph with "This" or "It".
 
### 1.3 Active vs passive voice
 
The book is **predominantly active voice** but uses passive deliberately in specific contexts:
 
- **Active (dominant):** "John guides the reader through the relevant interpretations." / "We use literacies here to describe..."
- **Passive for convention descriptions:** "Classroom management is generally seen as referring to..." / "Success is often seen in terms of results."
- **Passive for process descriptions:** "Your work is passed to another student who then applies the marking criteria."
- **Passive to foreground the concept over the actor:** "The principles that underpin sound practice in ethical research have been helpfully set out by the ESRC."
**Rule for generation:** Default to active voice. Use passive when the concept is more important than the actor, when describing conventions or norms, or when the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant. Ratio: approximately 75% active, 25% passive.
 
### 1.4 Hedging language
 
The book hedges **selectively**, not reflexively. Claims about student experience and practical advice are hedged; claims about academic conventions and established principles are confident.
 
**Hedged (uncertain/variable claims):**
- "might represent a significant academic challenge"
- "can be very difficult to overcome"
- "are less useful for the many students who already possess this knowledge"
- "could provide an important perspective on the issue"
- "is potentially problematic, since it might confuse rather than clarify"
- "is perhaps more emphasis placed on the author's name; but it's open to debate"
**Confident (established claims):**
- "Critical thought is inherent within the research involved in the process of engaging broadly with academic discourse."
- "A judgement is evaluative because it assesses the relative importance of two factors."
- "Paraphrasing is a vital part of effective writing."
- "Referencing is about much more than avoiding plagiarism."
**Hedging devices used:** may, might, can, could, often, usually, generally, perhaps, tends to, is likely to, we think, in our experience, it's worth noting that, it's fair to say that, it's important to note that.
 
**Rule for generation:** Hedge claims about individual student experience and variable outcomes. Be confident about established academic conventions, marking criteria definitions, and skill descriptions. Never hedge and then immediately undermine the hedge ("This might be important, but it's actually essential"). The default for instructional content is confident; reserve hedging for genuinely uncertain or variable claims.

## 2. Paragraph-level analysis
 
### 2.1 Paragraph length
 
**Observed range:** 2–7 sentences per paragraph, with 3–5 being the dominant pattern. Single-sentence paragraphs are extremely rare and used only for strong transitional statements.
 
**Rule for generation:** 3–5 sentences per paragraph. Permit 2-sentence paragraphs for emphasis or transition. Permit 6–7 sentence paragraphs for complex worked examples. Never exceed 7 sentences.
 
### 2.2 Paragraph structure
 
The book consistently uses a **topic sentence → development → implication** pattern:
 
1. **Topic sentence:** states the paragraph's main point
2. **Development:** evidence, example, qualification, or expansion
3. **Implication:** what this means for the student, often returning to the practical
**Example (Chapter 2):**
- Topic: "Success in relation to academic performance can take many forms because the learning experience associated with your degree programme has lots of different elements."
- Development: "We've already referred to the example of participating effectively in a workshop... Other tasks... A challenge facing all students..."
- Implication: "The point is that success isn't just about the marks you get for assessments; it's about how effectively you engage with all elements of the learning experience on your course."
**Rule for generation:** Every paragraph should have a discernible topic sentence (usually first, occasionally second). The final sentence should connect back to the student's practice or signal the next point. Avoid paragraphs that are purely descriptive with no implication.
 
### 2.3 Transitions between paragraphs
 
The book uses explicit paragraph-level transitions but avoids formulaic connectors. The transition is typically embedded in the first sentence of the new paragraph rather than being a standalone transition word.
 
**Observed transition strategies:**
- **Reference back:** "That said, success in relation to academic performance is for many students about how well they do on assessed tasks."
- **Contrast:** "It's one thing to make you aware of self-regulated learning, but it's another thing entirely for you to actually become a self-regulating learner."
- **Building on:** "In addition to the sort of questions we've just highlighted..."
- **Narrowing:** "Let's take a closer look at how John attempts to..."
- **Pivoting:** "So much for the principles behind effective referencing practice; how do you actually set out the references?"
**Transitions rarely used:** "Furthermore" at paragraph start, "Moreover" at paragraph start, "Additionally" at paragraph start (these appear mid-paragraph occasionally but not as paragraph openers).
 
**Rule for generation:** Transition between paragraphs by embedding the connection in the first sentence, not by prepending a standalone connector. Avoid "Furthermore," / "Moreover," / "Additionally," as paragraph openers. Prefer "That said," / "In addition to X," / "Beyond X," / constructions that reference the previous content specifically.

## 3. Voice and address
 
### 3.1 Person and perspective
 
The book uses a **first-person plural + direct second-person** pattern. The authors are "we"; the student is "you". The relationship is collegial: two experienced practitioners talking directly to the student.
 
**Observed patterns:**
- "We're not just talking about finding success" / "We use literacies here to describe"
- "Your understanding of success is also determined by the timeframe"
- "You need to appreciate why you are expected to do things"
- "As we've already said" / "As we see in the second part of this chapter"
**The book never uses:**
- Third-person reference to the student ("Students should..." / "The learner will...")
- First-person singular ("I think...")
- Impersonal "one" ("One might consider...")
**Adaptation for e-lesson content:** The e-lesson has no named authors, so "we" shifts to the implicit voice of the lesson. The critical element to preserve is the **direct "you"** and the **collegial relationship**; the lesson speaks *to* the student, not *about* them.
 
**Rule for generation:** Always use "you" and "your" for the student. Avoid "students should" / "the learner" / "one might". The implied author is a knowledgeable colleague, not an institution. "We" is acceptable for shared assumptions ("As we saw in Topic 3...") but not as a dominant pattern in e-lesson content where there is no named author.
 
### 3.2 Tone calibration
 
The book operates on a **spectrum within a single register**, not distinct modes:
 
- **Conversational end:** "Lots of Jess' friends have had a bit of a laugh about the formative assessment" / "You wouldn't arm a group of undergraduate history students with swords and shields"
- **Instructional middle:** "Paraphrasing refers to the practice of expressing the work of another author in your own words, without changing the meaning"
- **Analytical end:** "Since he is using the interpretations to support and develop that point, the work has a sharper critical edge"
The conversational touches (contractions, light humour, everyday analogies) prevent the instructional content from feeling like a textbook. The analytical passages demonstrate the seriousness of the ideas without shifting register entirely.
 
**Rule for generation:** Operate in the instructional middle as the default. Use conversational touches (contractions, brief analogies, acknowledgement of student feelings) to warm the tone, particularly at paragraph openings and in transitional moments. Use analytical register for worked examples and marking criteria discussion. Never shift fully into either extreme; the book never reads like a chat, nor like a journal article.
 
### 3.3 Contractions
 
The book uses contractions **freely and consistently**: it's, you're, we're, they're, don't, doesn't, won't, can't, that's, there's, we've, you've, you'll, isn't, aren't, we'll.
 
**Contractions NOT used:** ain't, gonna, wanna, could've (avoided, but "could have" used).
 
**Rule for generation:** Use contractions throughout. They are a core part of the register. Content without contractions will sound too formal for this voice. Exception: avoid contractions in formal reference text (e.g. Harvard references themselves).

## 4. Rhetorical strategies
 
### 4.1 Inclusive acknowledgement
 
The book consistently **acknowledges the student's starting position** before introducing new expectations. It names the feeling or challenge before providing the solution.
 
**Examples:**
- "This might be a really difficult thing for you to do since it can involve adopting an entirely different approach to learning."
- "Many new or less experienced students find participating in such assessment tasks challenging, simply because they haven't done anything like it before."
- "Some students, for example, who are either entirely new to studying at university or have come back into education after a long break away worry a great deal."
**Rule for generation:** Before introducing a new skill or expectation, acknowledge why it might feel unfamiliar or challenging. Do this in one sentence, not an extended empathy paragraph. Then move directly into the practical guidance.
 
### 4.2 Reframing
 
A distinctive feature: the book frequently **reframes a concept the student likely misunderstands**, using a "you probably think X, but actually Y" structure.
 
**Examples:**
- "Students usually see referencing in the context of plagiarism... Yet referencing is about much more than avoiding plagiarism."
- "This book is about more than simply 'study skills'... This book is about your personal development."
- "Success is, of course, about these things, but, at the same time, in higher education today, it's about much more than that."
- "Developing that critical edge doesn't necessarily involve any drastic steps. It's subtle and nuanced."
**Rule for generation:** Use reframing when introducing a concept students commonly misunderstand. State the common misconception briefly and without judgement, then pivot to the richer understanding. Use "but" or "yet" or "however" as the pivot, not "actually" (which can sound condescending).
 
### 4.3 Concrete before abstract
 
The book almost always **grounds abstract concepts in a specific example first**, then generalises.
 
**Examples:**
- John's search activity (concrete) → principles of critical thinking in research (abstract)
- Susie's writing exemplar (concrete) → distinction between passive and active approaches to sources (abstract)
- Natalie vs Jess completing a formative task (concrete) → principles of self-regulated learning (abstract)
**Rule for generation:** Introduce concepts through a specific example, scenario, or case study first. Generalise after. Avoid starting with a definition and then illustrating; start with the illustration and extract the principle.
 
### 4.4 Named student scenarios
 
The book uses **fictional named students** (John, Susie, Natalie, Jess) as recurring case study characters. They are introduced casually and referred to by first name. Their situations are realistic and specific.
 
**Characteristics:**
- Common first names, no surnames
- Described in terms of their approach to a task, not their demographics
- Presented neutrally even when their approach is flawed (Natalie's poor approach is described without mockery)
- Used to contrast approaches (Natalie vs Jess, Susie vs John)
**Rule for generation:** Use named student characters for scenario content. Common first names only. Describe their academic behaviour, not their background. When contrasting approaches, present the weaker approach without judgement; let the analysis do the work.
 
### 4.5 Direct questions to the reader
 
The book uses **genuine reflective questions**, not rhetorical ones. The questions expect the student to pause and think, not to agree with an obvious answer.
 
**Examples:**
- "How often do you use judgements in your writing?"
- "What tasks are you facing this week? Which tasks are the most challenging?"
- "How might Jess use this experience to bring about an improvement in her performance?"
- "Did you monitor your performance as you were completing the task?"
**Questions NOT used:** "Isn't it obvious that...?" / "Don't you think that...?" / "Wouldn't you agree that...?" (leading/rhetorical).
 
**Rule for generation:** Use direct questions to the reader only when a genuine reflective pause is intended. The question should be answerable from the student's own experience. Never use questions with an implied correct answer (those are rhetorical and patronising in this register).

## 5. Structural patterns
 
### 5.1 Chapter/section organisation
 
The book follows a consistent pattern per section:
 
1. **Orientation:** what this section is about and why it matters (1–2 paragraphs)
2. **Concept introduction:** usually through example first, then principle
3. **Worked example or case study:** concrete, named, specific
4. **Analysis of the example:** what it shows, connecting back to the concept
5. **Practical guidance:** what the student should do differently
6. **Reflective prompt or task:** inviting the student to connect to their own practice
**Rule for generation:** Follow this six-part flow within each topic section. Not every section needs all six parts, but the orientation → example → analysis → practical guidance arc should be recognisable.
 
### 5.2 Cross-referencing
 
The book **cross-references extensively** between chapters: "as we saw in Chapter 2", "we'll come back to this in Chapter 7", "this links to what we discussed earlier about self-efficacy". These are conversational, not formal.
 
**Rule for generation:** Cross-reference between topics and across pathways using conversational framing ("This connects to what you explored in Topic 3" / "The Finding & Using Sources pathway covers this in more detail"). Never use formal cross-reference notation ("See Section 3.2.1").

## 6. Punctuation and formatting
 
### 6.1 Punctuation conventions observed
 
| Convention | Observed pattern |
|---|---|
| Em dashes | **Not used.** The book uses commas, semicolons, colons, and parentheses instead. |
| En dashes | Used for page ranges (pp. 45–62) and number ranges. |
| Semicolons | Used freely to join related independent clauses. Frequent: approximately 1–2 per page. |
| Colons | Used to introduce lists, explanations, and elaborations. |
| Parentheses | Used for brief asides, definitions, and in-text references. |
| Comma usage | Serial (Oxford) comma **not consistently used**. Some lists use it, others do not. |
| Italics | Used for emphasis, book titles, key terms on first use, and Latin terms. |
| Bold | Reserved for headings and task labels. Not used for emphasis in running prose. |
| Quotation marks | Single quotes for 'terms being defined or problematised'. Double quotes for direct speech (rare) and quotations from sources. |
 
### 6.2 Formatting conventions
 
| Convention | Observed pattern |
|---|---|
| Headings | Hierarchical. Chapter → Section → Subsection. Active or topic-focused phrasing. |
| Bullet points | Used in tasks and reflective prompts. Rarely in running prose. |
| Numbered lists | Used for marking criteria, principles, and step-by-step guidance. |
| Block quotes | Used for extended writing exemplars and student scenarios (indented, italicised). |
| Tasks | Formatted as distinct blocks with "Task:" heading and question prompts. |
 
**Rule for generation:** No em dashes. Use semicolons freely for connected clauses. Use single quotes for terms being discussed as terms. Use italics for emphasis and key terms on first introduction. Do not use bold for emphasis in prose. Use bullet points only in explicit list contexts (checklists, task instructions), never in running paragraphs.

## 7. Vocabulary and register
 
### 7.1 Register band
 
The book operates in a **mid-register**: more formal than conversational English, less formal than an academic journal article. It sits in the same register band as quality broadsheet journalism (Guardian long reads, Economist features) or well-written professional non-fiction.
 
**Indicators of mid-register:**
- Uses "a lot" alongside "considerable"
- Uses "getting to grips with" alongside "acquiring competencies"
- Uses "tricky" alongside "problematic"
- Uses "a bit of a laugh" alongside "a significant academic challenge"
- Uses "needless to say" (conversational aside) in the same paragraph as "the evaluation phase of self-regulated learning" (technical)
### 7.2 Academic vocabulary handling
 
When the book introduces academic vocabulary, it **defines it immediately and conversationally**, often in parentheses or a trailing clause:
 
- "something called self-efficacy, which we'll come back to shortly"
- "et al. is an abbreviation for the Latin phrase et alia, meaning 'and others'"
- "its epistemology, of the genres through which the community interacts, and of the conventions that regulate these interactions"
**Rule for generation:** Define technical terms on first use. Use parentheses or a trailing clause for brief definitions. Use a glossary term block for terms that need fuller explanation. Never assume the reader knows a technical term; never over-explain a term that's been defined earlier in the lesson.
 
### 7.3 Words and phrases characteristically used
 
**Frequently observed:** therefore, in other words, in addition, however, that said, for example, let's, this is because, the point is, in our experience, it's worth, we think, you'll notice, it's fair to say, put simply, in what follows, as we've seen, note that, remember.
 
**Characteristically avoided:** henceforth, thus (rare, "therefore" preferred), notwithstanding, aforementioned, the student (as subject, always "you"), learner (as noun, always "you"), one (impersonal pronoun), shall.

## 8. Inclusivity and accessibility
 
### 8.1 Inclusive framing
 
The book addresses a **diverse student population** without drawing attention to specific demographics:
 
- "If you're new to studying at university... if you've already started" (covers both)
- "new or less experienced students" (avoids "struggling students")
- "students who are either entirely new to studying at university or have come back into education after a long break away"
- References to international students are respectful and practical
- Named characters use a range of common English first names
**Rule for generation:** Frame inclusively by covering multiple starting positions. Use "new or less experienced" rather than "weak" or "struggling". Acknowledge that students arrive via different routes without making any route seem deficient.
 
### 8.2 Accessibility of explanation
 
The book **explains the why before the how**. It consistently answers the unspoken question "why should I care about this?" before telling the student what to do.
 
**Rule for generation:** Before giving practical instruction, briefly state why this matters. One sentence is enough. "Understanding what your marker is actually looking for takes a lot of the mystery out of the task"; then explain the criteria.

## 9. Content-type-specific voice observations
 
### 9.1 Running prose (teaching content)
 
- Mid-register, varied sentence length, direct address
- Reframing structure common
- Concrete → abstract flow
- Hedging where appropriate, confident where established
### 9.2 Worked examples / case studies
 
- Named characters in italic block quotes
- Third-person narrative describing the student's actions
- Present tense for the narrative
- Analysis follows immediately, using the character's name
### 9.3 Reflective tasks / prompts
 
- Direct questions, second person
- Bullet-pointed sub-questions
- "Task:" label with clear heading
- Brief framing sentence before the questions
- Reassurance that there are no right answers where appropriate
### 9.4 Technical/reference content (referencing formats, etc.)
 
- More instructional, less conversational
- Step-by-step structure
- Explicit examples with formatting highlighted
- "Note that..." / "Remember that..." for key points
- Still uses "you" and contractions; does not shift to impersonal register
### 9.5 Feedback and assessment discussion
 
- Evaluative but not judgemental
- "The work demonstrates..." (about the work, not the student)
- Specific: references particular features of the exemplar
- Comparative where two exemplars are contrasted (Susie vs John)
- Never mocking or dismissive of the weaker example

## 10. Summary: Core voice rules derived from this audit
 
1. **Address:** Direct "you", collegial implied author, no third-person reference to students.
2. **Register:** Mid-register. Contractions used freely. Academic vocabulary defined on first use.
3. **Sentence rhythm:** Varied length (average 18–22 words). Short sentences for emphasis. Varied openers.
4. **Paragraph structure:** Topic → development → implication. 3–5 sentences typical.
5. **Transitions:** Embedded in first sentence of new paragraph, not standalone connectors.
6. **Hedging:** Selective; hedge variable/experiential claims, be confident on established conventions.
7. **Reframing:** "You probably think X, but actually Y" is a signature move.
8. **Concrete before abstract:** Ground concepts in examples first, generalise after.
9. **Inclusive acknowledgement:** Name the challenge before providing the solution.
10. **Questions:** Genuine reflective only. Never rhetorical or leading.
11. **Punctuation:** No em dashes. Semicolons and colons freely. Single quotes for discussed terms.
12. **Formatting:** No bold in prose. Italics for emphasis and key terms. Bullets only in list contexts.
13. **Feedback voice:** Evaluative about the work, never judgemental about the student.
14. **Named characters:** Common first names, described by behaviour not demographics, neutral even when flawed.
15. **Cross-referencing:** Conversational ("as we saw in Topic 3"), never formal notation.
