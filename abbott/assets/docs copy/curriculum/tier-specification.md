# Tier Specification

**Programme:** Academic Practice e-learning
**Source model:** *Excelling at University* (Dennis & Abbott, Routledge, 2026), reformulated from CQFW/FHEQ level descriptors
**Status:** v1.0 — validated against Academic Writing L01/L02 (F3, STEP 5). Two dimensions remain untested against real content: see the status note at the foot of this document.
**Purpose:** Defines what *introductory / intermediate / advanced* mean, so any author — human or AI, in any pathway or context — pitches a lesson at a consistent level, and so summative assessment holds equivalence across contexts.

**Relationship to other documents.** This is the *pedagogical/cognitive* counterpart to the Voice Specification's *linguistic* tier calibration (§16). The two together define a tier: this document sets the demand; §16 sets the register. It underpins the parity model in `scoring-and-summative-model.md` (parity rides on the tier) and feeds the per-component authoring specs (F4), some of whose guardrails are tier-dependent. It does not restate §16; where register, outcome verbs, or scenario voice are concerned, it points there.

---

## 1. How to use this document

An author picks this up to answer one question: *at what level do I pitch this lesson?* The procedure is in §8; the substance is in §5–§7. Read §2 first — the model has a few load-bearing ideas that make the rest coherent, and skipping them invites the exact drift this document exists to prevent.

This artefact records design decisions. Like `pathway-tier-mapping.md`, it is not written in the student voice and does not need to be; the Voice Specification governs lesson copy, not this planning document. Tables, lists, and direct reference-doc prose are used here deliberately for usability.

## 2. The model in one page

**Tiers are anchored to experience with the practice, not to credit level.** A student can be advanced at one practice and introductory at another. "Introductory" describes minimal prior experience *of this practice*, not first year of study; "advanced" describes fluent command *of this practice*, not final year. This is the decision that shapes everything else.

**The three tiers are a relative sequence, and the sequence is constant programme-wide.** Introductory → intermediate → advanced always describes the same *shape* of step-up — the same movement in demand, cognition, complexity, and autonomy. That constancy is what keeps dozens of lessons consistent. What the dimensions describe is the *relationship between the tiers*, not a fixed altitude.

**Altitude floats by pathway.** Because the anchor is the practice, the *absolute* demand of a tier differs between pathways. "Introductory" in Researching sits higher in absolute terms than "introductory" in Academic Writing (see the pathway-mapping note that Researching's introductory "is already a fairly capable student"). Each is nonetheless the correct entry point *to its own practice*. Each pathway fixes its altitude once, by anchoring its three tiers to three bands of the §16 voice gradient (see §3). This is the agreed reading (2) of the §16 relationship.

**Parity is held across contexts within a pathway-tier.** Criminology-introductory and sports-journalism-introductory (same pathway, same tier, different context) must be *equivalently demanding*, even though their subject matter differs. This is what `scoring-and-summative-model.md` means by "parity rides on the tier." Parity is **not** claimed across different pathways at the same tier — floating altitude makes that impossible and unnecessary. When authoring a summative slot for a new context, calibrate to *this tier's* demand (§7), not to a sibling context's specific questions.

**The dimensions.** A tier is defined across four *defining* dimensions plus one *parity-bearing* dimension, with one *per-pathway configuration* setting:

| | Dimension | Type | Where defined |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Assumed prior experience | Defining | §5 |
| D2 | Cognitive demand | Defining | §5 |
| D3 | Task complexity | Defining | §5 |
| D4 | Learner autonomy | Defining | §5 |
| D5 | Assessment phrasing & difficulty | Parity-bearing | §7 |
| — | Voice-band anchor | Per-pathway config | §3 |
| — | Outcome verbs | Cross-referenced to §16 | §6 |
| — | Scenario register | Cross-referenced to §16 | §6 |

"Knowledge," the fourth framework dimension, does not survive experience-anchoring as a standalone: its learner-facing half becomes D1 (what the learner brings), its material-facing half folds into D3 (how settled or contested the practice's forms are). Nothing is lost; it resolves into the two things authors can actually calibrate.

## 3. The voice-band anchor (set once per pathway)

This is the one setting each pathway fixes before its lessons are authored. It is what makes altitude float, and it is the hinge between this document and §16.

§16 calibrates six linguistic bands along a single gradient: **Foundation → Beginner → Intermediate → Advanced → Masters → Doctoral.** The programme delivers three pedagogical tiers. Each pathway maps its three tiers onto three *consecutive* bands of that gradient. The mapping sets the pathway's absolute altitude; the three-step *shape* defined in §5 rides on top of whichever three bands are chosen.

**Default anchor.** Most pathways anchor at the middle three bands:

| Pathway tier | Default §16 band |
|---|---|
| Introductory | Beginner |
| Intermediate | Intermediate |
| Advanced | Advanced |

**Skewed anchors.** A pathway whose entry point already assumes a capable student shifts the window up. Researching, which the pathway mapping notes "skews later than most," is the clear case:

| Pathway tier | Researching's §16 band (illustrative — confirm at authoring) |
|---|---|
| Introductory | Intermediate |
| Intermediate | Advanced |
| Advanced | Masters |

**Rule for choosing the anchor.** Ask where the *introductory* tier of the pathway sits: what does a learner with no prior experience *of this practice* actually need to be able to read and do on day one? If that entry point assumes the reader is already a competent academic writer or researcher, the window shifts up. Anchor the introductory tier to the §16 band whose register and outcome verbs match that entry point, then take the next two bands in sequence. Record the choice in the pathway's entry in `pathway-tier-mapping.md`; it is a per-pathway decision, made once, and it governs register, outcome verbs (§6), and scenario voice for every lesson in that pathway.

**Consequence to hold in mind.** Because the window floats, the *absolute* cognitive floor and ceiling of a tier are set by its anchored band, while the *relative* three-step progression in §5 stays constant. A skewed pathway's "introductory" therefore involves higher-order operations than a default pathway's "introductory" — correctly so. §5 describes the shape of the climb; §3 sets which slope you are climbing.

## 4. The dimensions, and why each is separate

**D1 — Assumed prior experience.** The anchor of the model: what the learner brings. Everything else follows from it. Kept distinct because it is the thing an author must decide *first* — it sets what may be assumed and what must be taught.

**D2 — Cognitive demand.** The *type* of thinking required, in Bloom-family terms. Kept separate from complexity because the two genuinely diverge: a structurally simple task can demand high-order thinking, and a structurally messy task can demand only recognition.

**D3 — Task complexity.** The *structure* of the task and the maturity of the material — how many factors are in play, how well-defined the problem is, and whether the practice's forms are settled procedure or contested judgement. This is where the material half of "knowledge" lives.

**D4 — Learner autonomy.** How much the lesson scaffolds versus how much the learner directs, including the character of feedback (feedback that shows, feedback that points, feedback that opens a dialogue). Folded in here rather than given its own dimension because feedback character is an expression of autonomy, not a separate axis.

**D5 — Assessment phrasing & difficulty.** Treated as its own dimension in §7 because it is the parity-bearing one: it is the concrete standard an author calibrates a summative context-slot against, and therefore the teeth of the equivalence model.

Outcome verbs (§6) and scenario register (§6) are not restated as dimensions here; they are governed by §16 via the pathway's anchor, so that they shift with altitude automatically and stay single-sourced.

## 5. The three tiers across the defining dimensions

The matrix below describes the *relative* three-step progression. Read it against the pathway's anchored §16 window (§3): the shape is constant; the absolute register and verb range come from the anchor.

| | **Introductory** | **Intermediate** | **Advanced** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D1 — Assumed prior experience** | No prior experience of the practice assumed. May have met it in passing but has not done it deliberately. Teach from the practice's first principles; assume nothing about the practice itself. | A working foundation in the practice is in place. The learner can perform its basic form; build on it without re-teaching basics. Developing an academic identity around the practice. | Fluent command of the practice's ordinary forms. Competence assumed. Ready for its complex, contested, and self-directed use. |
| **D2 — Cognitive demand** | Recognition, recall, description, and guided application dominate. The work is *understand and identify*. | Analysis and evaluation become the main work; the learner applies the practice to new instances and takes it apart to see how it works. | Synthesis, critique, and judgement. The learner constructs, justifies, and interrogates; draws parts together in service of an argument. |
| **D3 — Task complexity** | Well-defined tasks in familiar contexts. One variable at a time. Settled procedure with a better and worse way. | Multi-step tasks with more than one factor in play; ambiguity introduced deliberately. Procedure gives way to informed choice. | Open, ambiguous, multi-factor problems. Contested judgement with no single right answer. Novel or unpredictable instances. |
| **D4 — Learner autonomy** | High scaffolding. Worked examples, step-by-step support, models shown before practice. Feedback confirms and explains — it shows the way. Frequent low-stakes checks. | Reduced scaffolding. Guided practice with prompts rather than full models. Feedback *points* toward the answer and asks the learner to apply the principle, rather than giving it. | Minimal scaffolding. Open, self-directed tasks. Feedback prompts self-evaluation and opens a dialogue rather than resolving it. The learner sets some of their own criteria. |

**Reading the matrix as an author.** Pitch every element of a lesson to the *column* for your tier, across all four rows at once. A common failure is pitching one dimension correctly and another wrongly — e.g. advanced cognitive demand (D2) wrapped in introductory scaffolding (D4), which produces a lesson that asks for critique but holds the learner's hand through it. The tier is the *whole column*, not any single row.

**What is constant across tiers, in the D4 row's feedback.** The two-stage feedback convention (confirm the response, then explain the reason) and warm, direct address in formative feedback hold at every tier. What varies by tier is *directiveness*, not the convention itself: introductory feedback states the answer and why; intermediate feedback points to where the answer lies; advanced feedback asks a question that leads the learner to it. Vocabulary also varies by tier, per the pathway's anchored §16 band. This settles a prior conflict: `component-mapping-artefact.md`'s M46 ("feedback voice is consistent across tiers; only vocabulary adjusts") is obsolete, superseded by this clarification (FV-1, 20 September 2026).

## 6. Outcome verbs and scenario register (via §16)

**Outcome verbs.** Do not select verbs from a list held here. Draw them from the §16 band this pathway-tier anchors to (§3). A default-anchored pathway takes its introductory verbs from §16's Beginner band, intermediate from Intermediate, advanced from Advanced; a skewed pathway takes them from its shifted window. §16 is authoritative and holds the per-band verb families. Two rules govern their use:

1. **Match the verb to the cognitive-demand row (D2).** The verb in a learning outcome must name the thinking the tier actually asks for. An introductory outcome built on "evaluate" mis-states the demand even if the lesson body is pitched correctly.
2. **Avoid the vague verbs §15.8 prohibits** — "understand," "know," "appreciate," "be aware of" — at every tier. Name the observable operation.

**Scenario register.** The *structural* character of example scenarios is set in D3 (single clean situation → multi-factor ambiguous one). How a scenario *sounds* — its vocabulary, sentence length, and framing — is governed by §16's tier-specific adjustments (the examples and vocabulary rows), via the anchor. Set the structure here; take the voice from §16.

## 7. Assessment phrasing & difficulty (the parity standard)

This section is the concrete standard for summative equivalence. Per `scoring-and-summative-model.md`, a summative assessment is calibrated to its *tier's* demand, whatever context it sits in: criminology-introductory and sports-journalism-introductory summatives differ in subject but must be equivalently demanding. Calibrate a new context's summative slot to the tier row below — do not match it question-for-question to a sibling context.

| | **Introductory** | **Intermediate** | **Advanced** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Question form** | Recognition and short-application. "Identify…", "Which of these…", "Describe…", "Match…". | Applied and analytical. "Analyse…", "Compare…", "Apply X to…", "What would you do when…". | Open, synthetic, justification-demanding. "Evaluate…", "To what extent…", "Justify…", "Construct…". |
| **Stimulus** | Short and clean. One concept per item. | A short scenario with more than one factor to weigh. | A rich or ambiguous scenario; may span several parts. |
| **Discrimination** | Distractors clearly distinguishable to a learner who has grasped the concept. | Distractors plausible; require analysis, not just recall, to reject. | The demand is in the construction and justification, not in trap-spotting. |
| **What it tests** | That each taught concept has landed. | That the learner can apply and analyse in a new instance. | Synthesis *across* the lesson's parts, not recall of each part (the end-of-tier synthesis principle in the scoring model). |

**Parity check for a summative slot.** Before finalising, ask: *would a learner who can pass the sibling context's summative also pass this one, and vice versa?* If one is markedly easier — simpler stem, cleaner stimulus, lower-order verb — it has drifted off the tier and must be re-pitched to the row above. The subject may differ freely; the demand may not.

## 8. Authoring procedure

1. **Identify the pathway and tier** of the lesson.
2. **Read the pathway's voice-band anchor** (§3, recorded in `pathway-tier-mapping.md`). This fixes register and verb range.
3. **Pitch every element to the tier column** in §5 — all four defining dimensions at once (see the failure note in §5).
4. **Draw outcome verbs** from the anchored §16 band (§6); match each verb to the cognitive-demand row.
5. **Set scenario structure** from D3; take scenario voice from §16.
6. **If the lesson is summative**, calibrate the assessment to the §7 tier row and run the parity check.
7. **Apply the Voice Specification** throughout for register, rhythm, and the content-type rules (§15). This document sets *what* the demand is; §16 and the rest of the Voice Spec set *how it sounds*.

## 9. A worked illustration from the pilot

The clearest illustration of how the dimensions work comes from a real introductory lesson — "Understanding the task" (Academic Writing, L02) — because it shows why cognitive demand (D2) and autonomy (D4) must be read as separate dimensions.

That lesson's worked example has the character Rhian reason through an essay question, spotting that the word "explain" commits her to causes and that "rising" commits her to a trend over time rather than a snapshot. That is genuine analytical work — the kind of move that, *demanded of a learner unaided*, would sit at intermediate. Yet the lesson is correctly an introductory lesson, because the sophisticated move is only ever *shown*: it is a model on the page (high scaffold, D4), and the single thing the learner is then asked to do is *identify* the steer in the question (recognition, D2). The demand stays at introductory; the scaffold carries the sophistication.

The lesson would look mis-tiered if it were judged on a single blurred axis of "how sophisticated is the content." It is only legible as introductory because the specification separates *what the learner is asked to do* from *how much the lesson holds their hand while showing them*. This is the "whole column" point in §5 in practice: pitch demand and scaffold together, and an introductory lesson can show an expert move without becoming an intermediate one.

To see the same teaching move climb the tiers: at **intermediate**, the learner would be given a question and asked to *analyse* its steer and keywords themselves, with a prompt but no worked model. At **advanced**, they would *evaluate* a question whose steer is genuinely ambiguous, *justify* the reading they commit to, and account for what that reading rules out — with feedback that opens the judgement rather than settling it. The subject holds constant; demand, task-openness, and the withdrawal of the scaffold climb together.

## 10. Boundaries — what this document does and does not govern

- **This document governs:** the demand level of a lesson — assumed experience, cognitive demand, task complexity, autonomy, and assessment difficulty — and the equivalence standard for summative parity across contexts.
- **§16 (Voice Specification) governs:** register, sentence-level calibration, outcome-verb families, and scenario voice. This document points to §16; it does not restate it.
- **`scoring-and-summative-model.md` governs:** the mechanism of scoring, pass/fail, and the formative→summative structure. This document supplies the *calibration standard* that model's parity rests on.
- **`pathway-tier-mapping.md` governs:** which content sits at which tier in each pathway, and records each pathway's voice-band anchor.
- **Component authoring specs (F4) govern:** per-component JSON shape and field-level guardrails, some of which are tier-dependent and will cite this document.

## Status note — validation and open caveats

**v1.0.** Validated against the Academic Writing introductory pilot (L01 "The writing cycle", L02 "Understanding the task") on 10 July 2026. Both lessons sit cleanly at introductory across assumed prior experience, cognitive demand, task complexity, and learner autonomy (D1–D4). The pilot also confirmed the value of separating D2 from D4 (see §9).

Two dimensions could not be exercised by the pilot and remain validated-by-design only, to be tested against real content when it exists:

1. **Assessment phrasing and parity (D5 / §7)** is untested. The pilot is formative-only, so there was no summative assessment to calibrate. The first real test is the introductory tier's end-of-tier summative (backlog C2); §7 should be revisited then and this note updated.
2. **Outcome-verb compliance (§6)** is untested. The pilot lessons are prose drafts with no explicit learning outcomes, so verb selection could only be inferred from the demanded tasks (which are introductory-clean). When outcomes are authored, they must draw from the §16 Beginner verb set and avoid intermediate verbs (interpret, analyse) even where a worked example models that thinking. One watch-point surfaced: L02's guidance phrase "work out what the steer is asking" brushes an interpretive verb — acceptable as teaching prose, but a flag for outcome authoring.

Neither caveat blocks use of this specification for authoring now; both are limits of the *test*, not faults in the spec.
