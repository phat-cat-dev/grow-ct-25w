# Authoring multiple-choice questions — balance rules (for the agent)

Generic rules the agent follows whenever it writes MCQ items — the formative
`check` (les-01) and the summative items (wra-03). The goal: a learner who knows
the material answers correctly, and a learner who does not **cannot** guess from
the *shape* of the options. Apply these before treating any MCQ as done.

## The failure these rules prevent

The commonest flaw is the **give-away correct answer**: the right option is the
longest, most detailed, most carefully qualified one, and the distractors are
short and blunt. Test-wise learners pick "the long, careful one" and are right
without understanding. Real example caught in L01:

> *Which best describes how learning at university differs from school?*
> - You rely on your tutor's notes as the only source you need. *(blunt)*
> - University is the same as school, only with harder material. *(blunt)*
> - You mainly take in and give back what you are told. *(blunt)*
> - **You take part in making and testing knowledge, not just receiving it.** *(long, nuanced — obviously the answer)*

The correct option gives itself away by being the only descriptive, hedged one.

## Rules

1. **Length parity.** All options are about the same length (within ~25%). The
   correct option must **not** be the longest or the most elaborated. If the
   right answer needs nuance to be true, either trim it or raise the distractors
   to the same level of detail.
2. **Parallel structure.** Every option shares grammatical form, tense and
   opening style, and completes the stem the same way. No option stands out by
   its grammar.
3. **Equal specificity.** Don't let only the correct option carry precise,
   qualified wording ("…, not just X") while distractors are flat. Match the
   specificity across all four, or remove it from the key.
4. **Plausible distractors.** Each distractor is a *real misconception* a learner
   at this tier might actually hold — drawn from common errors about the concept,
   not filler or jokes. A distractor nobody would pick is a wasted option.
5. **Homogeneous options.** All four are the same kind of thing (all definitions,
   or all actions, or all consequences) at the same grain. No category outlier.
6. **No cue words.** Avoid absolutes in distractors (always, never, only, all)
   that flag them as false, and soft qualifiers in the key (usually, often, tends
   to) that flag it as true. Keep the hedging even across options.
7. **No structural tells.** No "all/none of the above". Don't cluster the correct
   answer in one position across a set; vary it (Adapt may also shuffle, but
   author as if it will not). Keep grammatical agreement (a/an, singular/plural)
   working for *every* option so none is grammatically ruled out.
8. **Stem does the work.** Put the shared wording and the actual question in the
   stem; keep options short. Don't repeat the same phrase in every option.
9. **One defensibly correct answer.** Exactly one option is right; the rest are
   clearly wrong *to someone who knows the material*, yet tempting to someone who
   does not. If two could be argued correct, rewrite.
10. **Feedback gives the reason** (house rule): never a bare "Correct"/"Wrong" —
    confirm and say *why*, and for the key say what made the distractors wrong.

## Quick self-check before "done"

- Cover the correct answer: do the distractors still look like genuine options?
- Rank the options by length — is the longest the key? If so, fix.
- Could a learner who skipped the lesson pick the key from shape alone? If yes,
  it is not balanced yet.
- Are all four the same kind of statement, equally specific, equally hedged?

Applies at every tier; the *content* of the distractors gets more subtle at
higher tiers, but the balance rules are constant.
